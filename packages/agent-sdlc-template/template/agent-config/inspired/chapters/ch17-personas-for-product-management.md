# Chapter 17: Personas for Product Management

## Premise
A persona is an archetype description of an imaginary but very plausible user,
capturing the behaviors, attitudes, and goals of a real type of person learned
from interviewing real users. For a product manager, personas are a forcing
function for the hardest choices — especially deciding who the next release is
*not* for.

## Key Principles
- Product management is all about choices, and you have to make most of them
  right. Personas are one of the best tools for making the hard ones.
- There is no single "user." Your job is to deeply understand the major types
  of users — current and future — and what they're trying to do.
- Personas are typically used by designers (for goals and online behaviors) and
  by marketers (for messaging, advertising, emotional appeals). Both uses are
  valid, and both are useful to the product manager — but the PM's use is
  distinct: it's a prioritization tool.
- Personas only deliver their potential if the product manager is deeply
  involved in their creation and prioritization — and especially in the user
  interviews that feed them.
- Personas are not a substitute for talking face-to-face with real target users
  and testing designs on real users. They're a theory until validated.
- Like product principles, personas rally the team around a common vision so
  the thousands of in-the-weeds calls get made consistently without the PM in
  the room.

## Practices
- Create personas as early in the discovery process as possible. Don't wait
  until designers drag them in late.
- Make persona creation a collaboration between product management, interaction
  design, and (if you have one) user research. Do not delegate this task.
- Attend every user interview, the same way you attend every usability test.
  The deep understanding of the target user comes only from talking with as
  many users and customers as possible.
- Focus each release on a single primary persona. The release will still be
  valuable and usable to others, but your job is to do a great job for one
  target user.
- Use the persona as the priority filter: if a feature is critical for "Mary"
  (the chosen primary persona for this release), it's in; if it's for "Sam,"
  it's out for now.
- Recruit a range of users for prototype testing — not only your primary
  persona — since your shipped product won't have the luxury of being used only
  by its target.

## Pitfalls
- Creating personas but refusing to make the hard call about which persona
  takes priority. "Our product is for everyone" only deludes the team and
  produces a muddled mess that pleases no one.
- Confusing yourselves with your customers — one of the most common product
  team mistakes. Personas help shine a light on it.
- Building personas from assumptions and stereotypes without verifying with
  real users. Treat your initial impressions as a theory until interviews
  prove them out.
- Trying to please end-users, managers, and administrators all in one release
  and ending up with a muddled mess — when sometimes the real answer is a
  separate user experience.

## Notable Frameworks / Definitions
- **Persona (aka user profile).** An archetype description of an imaginary
  but very plausible user that personifies the behaviors, attitudes, and goals
  of a real type of customer. The technique was first described in 1998 in
  Alan Cooper's *The Inmates Are Running the Asylum*; for a detailed treatment,
  see *About Face 3*.
- **Primary persona.** The single persona a release is for. Just as important
  as deciding who a release is for is deciding who it is not for.
- **PM vs. design vs. marketing use of personas.** All three use the same
  artifact differently — designers care most about the user's goals and online
  behaviors; marketers care about reaching the target and appealing to
  emotions; product management uses personas to prioritize.
