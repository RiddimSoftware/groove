# Chapter 32: Beware of Specials

## Premise
A "special" is a deal where a large customer or partner offers a big check on the condition that you build into the product exactly what they say. They look like easy revenue — the customer is essentially underwriting features you'd build anyway — but they confuse customer requirements with product requirements, fragment the product, and slow the company until even small releases take months. The product manager's job is to keep the product general-purpose and tailorable, not customized one buyer at a time.

## Key Principles
- "One of the surest ways to derail a product company is to confuse customer requirements with product requirements."
- Customers cannot describe the product they need: (1) it's extremely difficult to know what you need until you see it; (2) customers don't know what's possible; (3) customers don't interact with each other to identify common themes.
- Even when those issues don't apply, the right question is the opportunity cost: by pursuing this special now, what work are you delaying, and what is the business cost of that delay?
- Your job is to meet the needs of a *broad range* of customers — that's what distinguishes a product company from a custom software shop.
- Specials are contractual lock-in: if the market shifts a year out, contractual obligations to support specific behaviors will prevent you from adapting.
- Every version of the product has to be built, maintained, tested, released, documented, and supported — "it doesn't take too many specials to weigh down a company to the point where it takes them months to do even the smallest release."
- Most customers don't actually *want* to be on a custom version of your software; they want to be on your mainstream product — the one that gets the most attention, support, and improvement.
- Custom software shops are valuable and legitimate — they're just a fundamentally different business than commercial product software, and the two shouldn't be mixed.
- Avoiding specials takes corporate discipline; that leadership comes from the CEO, but the product manager can do a lot to help.

## Practices
- When a customer describes their problem in terms of a specific solution, work with them to tease out the underlying core issues and needs — there may be approaches they would like even better.
- Keep the product general-purpose, and make it tailorable / customizable / extensible by the customer or by a solutions provider.
- Have a short list of system integrator / solution-provider partners ready to tailor the product to specific customer needs, so requests for customization don't have to go into your roadmap.
- Partner with solution providers so the customer doesn't have to manage two relationships or worry about finger-pointing if things go wrong.
- In consumer Internet, treat advertiser deals as a product decision, not a sales decision: when you have a strategy and a clear role for advertisers, you can find synergistic arrangements rather than ones that distract from or damage the user experience.

## Pitfalls
- Cash-strapped CEOs accepting specials because "the customer is underwriting features we'd build anyway" — and then carrying the maintenance cost forever.
- Confusing being "market driven" with being directed by a single large customer's solution sketch.
- Letting a sales rep — or, in consumer Internet, an ad salesperson — bring a seven-figure deal directly to the CEO with site-integration or feature strings attached.
- Accepting advertising arrangements whose main objective is to move traffic *off* your site when that conflicts with your strategy.
- Accumulating enough specials that release cadence collapses from weeks to months.
- Using a "requirements management" tool that institutionalizes customer-requirement-as-product-requirement — in the worst case it locks in misguided practice; in the best case it duplicates a Wiki.

## Notable Frameworks / Definitions
- **Special, defined.** "A special is when a company gets a big check from a prospective customer or partner with the condition that you build into your product exactly what they say."
- **Customer requirements vs. product requirements.** Customer requirements describe what one specific customer wants; product requirements describe what a wide range of customers needs. Specials collapse the distinction, and the PM's job is to keep them separate.
- **Two-step defense against specials.** (1) Help the customer move from their proposed solution back to the core problem, so a mainstream-product solution becomes possible. (2) Keep the product general-purpose but tailorable, and route customer-specific work to a system integrator / solution-provider partner.
- **On requirements-management tools.** They make it extremely easy to fall into the trap of confusing customer requirements with product requirements; a Wiki (or Word / Excel) is usually sufficient and doesn't distract from the actual product thinking.
