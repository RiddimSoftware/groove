# Chapter 30: Succeeding in Large Companies

## Premise
Large organizations are structurally risk-averse — they have more to lose — and many of them run on matrix management with shared resources, which means getting a product designed, built, and launched is fundamentally a job of mobilizing other people. The way to succeed isn't to fight the culture but to learn how decisions are really made, build relationships in advance, and use the company's mass as leverage rather than treating it as an obstacle.

## Key Principles
- Don't try to change the culture — learn it, accept it, embrace it. If you want to succeed in your company, you'll need to "learn to love it."
- Decisions in large companies are often made by one key person (or a few), regardless of the formal decision process; figure out who that is and how they decide (demo, market data, customer commitments, testimonials).
- Large companies run on relationships and shared resources — going it alone is a startup mindset, not a big-company one.
- Public opposition is hard to reverse: once someone has opposed your position publicly, they cannot easily switch — so build consensus *before* important meetings, not during them.
- Information is treated as currency in large companies and hoarded; sharing it freely is both ethically correct and tactically smart.
- The benefits of shipping from a large company — press, analyst, partner, customer, and user attention — cannot be bought at any price by a small company, which is why learning to use those assets pays off.

## Practices
- **Learn how decisions are really made.** Look past the formal process for who actually has the buy-off, and learn how that person decides.
- **Build relationships before you need them.** Map every person across the company you might have to depend on; introduce yourself, ask how to work with them, and look for ways to help their job — well before you need their help.
- **Long live skunk works.** Beg forgiveness rather than ask permission: build a prototype with like-minded friends; if it's a good idea, resources will line up once they can see it works.
- **Just get it done.** When suitable resources aren't available — even in a company of thousands — find contractor funding, call in favors, or pitch in yourself. Be willing to write the QA, support, sales-training, or technical-writing deliverable yourself rather than fight the process.
- **Pick your battles.** The most effective people in a large org have more friends than enemies. Fight only over outcomes that truly matter; fight for your product, not against another person; don't back adversaries into a corner.
- **Build consensus before important meetings.** Talk one-on-one to every attendee beforehand so concerns are surfaced privately and addressed directly; the meeting itself is the place for visible support, not for unresolved debate.
- **Be smart about time.** Triage meetings ruthlessly; trust colleagues to do their jobs; protect time for the work that actually matters — strategy, roadmap, prototype, competition, and talking to actual users and customers.
- **Share information** generously rather than hoarding it.
- **Put your manager to work.** Leverage your manager's relationships and standing; do your homework and bring them the information they need to make your case to others.
- **Evangelize.** Continuously spread the word — vision, strategy, prototype demos, customer feedback — to everyone even remotely connected to your product. Internal sales never stops.

## Pitfalls
- Trying to change the corporate culture instead of operating within it.
- Treating an organization chart as a decision-making map when one or two people actually own the call.
- Lone-wolf product managers who try to ship without first building cross-functional relationships.
- Pushing ideas only through formal channels (PowerPoint deck up the chain) when a prototype + skunk-works coalition is what the company will actually respond to.
- Letting a week of nonstop meetings consume the time you need for strategy, the next prototype, and user conversations.
- Hoarding information as if it were power — you have more to gain by sharing it.
- Burning bridges over the wrong fight, especially one that's against a *person* rather than for the *product*.

## Notable Frameworks / Definitions
- **Two underlying dynamics of large companies.** (1) They are *risk-averse* — they have more to lose, and most of the friction you encounter is a mechanism for protecting what they have. (2) They run on *matrix management and shared resources*: key roles (design, engineering, QA, site operations, marketing) belong to pools that your project must staff from, chosen for cost savings rather than effectiveness.
- **The Top 10 techniques for succeeding in a large company.** (1) Learn how decisions are really made. (2) Build relationships before you need them. (3) Long live skunk works. (4) Just get it done. (5) Pick your battles. (6) Build consensus before important meetings. (7) Be smart about how you spend your time. (8) Share information. (9) Put your manager to work. (10) Evangelize.
- **David Weiden's closing line.** "Most people wander around in the dark and bitch about it being dark, instead of learning where the light switches are."
