# Chapter 36: Usability vs. Aesthetics

## Premise
A good product needs both interaction design and visual design — they are
distinct disciplines, and very few people are excellent at both. Teams that
treat "designer" as one role, or that hand design to the PM or UI engineer,
predictably ship sites that are either pretty but unusable or usable but ugly.
Both halves matter because visual design is how the product actually creates the
emotional response that drives use.

## Key Principles
- Interaction design and visual design are different disciplines requiring
  different training and skills — like product management vs. product
  marketing. A team needs both skill sets, ideally as two people, occasionally
  as one rare hybrid.
- Visual design is not superficial fluff. It plays a direct role in creating the
  emotion that inspires people to buy and use a product. The same functionality
  shown as wireframes vs. as a well-designed visual will produce dramatically
  different user reactions.
- The most common failure mode is no designer at all — the product manager or
  the UI engineer designs the site by default. This is still the norm in many
  enterprise companies.
- Visual design firms and interaction design firms each show a characteristic
  weakness in their own websites: beautiful but hard-to-read for visual firms;
  easy to navigate but boring and primitive for interaction firms. Neither is
  sufficient on its own.

## Practices
- Staff both an interaction designer and a visual designer on the design team.
  If your hybrid is genuinely talented at both, count yourself lucky — most
  "designers" are not.
- Have the PM work closely with both designers to define the product —
  functionality and user experience are inseparable.
- Don't ask the UI engineer or the PM to do the visual or interaction design as
  a side responsibility; the result will look like it.
- Treat performance, bug-freeness, and ad density as user experience concerns
  too — a site that performs like a dog or is riddled with ads will undermine
  even good design.

## Pitfalls
- Assuming "we hired a designer" covers both interaction and visual design when
  the hire is strong at only one.
- Dismissing visual polish as unnecessary because "what matters is functionality
  and value proposition."
- Letting the PM or a UI engineer design the site because no real designer is
  on staff — common in enterprise software, and visible in the result.
