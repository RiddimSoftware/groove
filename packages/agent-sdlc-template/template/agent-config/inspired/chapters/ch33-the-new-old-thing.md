# Chapter 33: The New Old Thing

## Premise
Most big product wins are not entirely new categories — they are dramatically
better incarnations of something old. Google and the iPod entered crowded,
"mature" markets and redefined them by combining a clear understanding of where
existing products fall short with what newly available technology now makes
possible.

## Key Principles
- The press obsesses over "what's the next new thing?" but the next big thing is
  usually a new version of an old thing, done so much better, faster, or cheaper
  that it redefines the category.
- A mature market is not a closed market — Google entered a search market with
  dozens of incumbents (AltaVista, Infoseek, Snap) and won by being consistently
  useful; Apple entered an MP3 market with over a hundred players and won by
  being dramatically better.
- What is possible is always changing. Something infeasible today may be feasible
  tomorrow as new technologies emerge.
- Today's applications are tomorrow's foundation. The browser was once just an
  app for viewing pages; it became the foundation for eBay, Skype, PayPal, and
  later Facebook became a foundation for a new wave of apps.
- Great product managers combine what is desirable with what is just now
  possible — neither side alone is enough.

## Practices
- Understand your target market and identify where current products fall short.
  Usability testing — including testing on competitors' products — is the
  author's favorite technique for surfacing these shortfalls.
- Stay actively on top of relevant new technologies and continuously ask how
  each one might be applied to the problems your product is trying to solve.
- Use frustration with existing products as opportunity signal: cell phones that
  drop calls, home computers parents can't administer alone — every consistently
  annoying product is an opening for someone to do it better.

## Pitfalls
- Believing you must invent an entirely new market to do something big.
- Dismissing a market as "mature" and therefore closed to new entrants.
- Tracking only what is desirable, or only what is technically possible —
  ignoring the intersection where winning products live.
- Assuming today's foundations are fixed; today's app layer becomes tomorrow's
  platform layer.
