# Chapter 29: Innovating in Large Companies

## Premise
Large companies become risk-averse as they grow — they have more to lose — and that conservatism, not lack of talent, is what suppresses innovation. The two biggest determinants of whether you can innovate in a big company are its culture and your manager; the rest of the chapter catalogs the five mechanisms that consistently produce innovation anyway.

## Key Principles
- Innovation can happen in large companies — it just runs into structural risk aversion that smaller companies don't have.
- The single biggest leverage point on your ability to innovate is your corporate culture and your immediate manager.
- Many of the best product ideas come from the bottom up, not from strategic planning or the executive team.
- Innovation is rarely about solving an entirely new problem — it's usually about solving an existing problem in a new way, which is why watching users struggle with current solutions is so productive.
- Every system has an *implementation model*; users have a *conceptual model*; mismatches between the two are innovation opportunities.
- Acquisition is a legitimate innovation channel, not a failure of the product team — large companies can let dozens of startups test the waters and acquire the ones that worked.
- Drucker: "Every organization needs one core competence: innovation."

## Practices
- **20% rule.** Let people spend 20% of their time on projects of their choice; make sure it covers product managers and interaction designers, not just engineers.
- **Skunk works.** When you can't get permission, flesh an idea out into a prototype on the side; once you can show it works, funding lines up surprisingly fast. (Continue doing your day job while you do it.)
- **Observation.** Watch and listen as actual target users — not early adopters, not anyone from your company — try to use your current product or a competitor's, ideally in their native habitat (office, home, mall). Bring along an engineer to map frustrations onto available technology.
- **User experience design.** Relax technical constraints for a moment and ask what the ideal experience would be — what tasks could be eliminated entirely, not just made more efficient? Look for places the product reflects the implementation model when it should reflect the user's conceptual model.
- **Acquisition.** Build relationships with interesting related startups before any deal is on the table; many acquired companies didn't take the highest bid, they took the company they wanted to work with.

## Pitfalls
- Assuming innovation only happens at startups and that big companies can only copy or acquire.
- Pushing ideas through "the proper channels" via PowerPoint when a prototype would get funded.
- Filtering observation sessions through internal staff or early adopters instead of the actual target user.
- Caring only about whether the software is usable, and not whether it's solving the problem the user actually has.
- Viewing acquisition as a product team failure rather than as a deliberate innovation strategy.
- Forgetting that the company owns the IP of anything built on the job — skunk works is for the company's products, not your unrelated startup idea.

## Notable Frameworks / Definitions
- **Five techniques for innovating in a large company.** (1) Innovation via the 20% rule. (2) Innovation via skunk works. (3) Innovation via observation. (4) Innovation via user experience design. (5) Innovation via acquisition.
- **Implementation model vs. conceptual model.** Every product is built on an implementation model; users approach it with a conceptual model of how the problem should work. Frustration happens when the product surfaces the implementation model. Each incongruence is an opportunity for innovation or significant product improvement.
