# Chapter 15: Charter User Programs

## Premise
Launching without strong reference customers is a huge red flag — it usually
means the product is bad or not ready. A Charter User Program solves two
problems at once: it gives the product manager deep, ongoing access to a small
set of real target customers during development, and it produces those
referenceable customers at launch.

## Key Principles
- Reference customers at launch dramatically reduce the risk a prospect feels
  buying your product. Without them, no amount of creative marketing or clever
  sales tactics will carry you far.
- One or two reference sites is its own warning — it suggests you've built a
  custom solution, not a product.
- Charter users are development partners, not customers. Treat them as
  colleagues; the relationships can last decades.
- If you can't recruit charter users, your problem probably isn't important
  enough to build a business around. This is one of the earliest reality checks
  available.
- You cannot build products users love without deep understanding of those
  users — and you don't get that without direct, face-to-face interaction.
  Surveys, focus-group reports, and sales-engineer summaries are not substitutes.
- The product manager must be the one interacting with users. Don't abdicate
  this responsibility, even when marketing, sales, or customer service all want
  to "help" by filtering it.

## Practices
- Recruit 8-10 charter customers at the start of the project, aiming to finish
  with at least 6 happy, live, referenceable customers from your target market.
  For consumer services, expand to 10-15.
- Strike an explicit deal. Customers get: early and significant product input,
  early access, and significantly reduced cost (if any). You get: ongoing
  access to their users and offices, periodic group sessions at your office,
  prompt deployment and feedback on test versions, and a public reference at
  launch if they're happy.
- Do not let charter users pay in advance — that turns the relationship into a
  custom build. Take their money after delivering a product they love.
- Cap the program at about 10 customers; you can't work closely with more.
  Create a separate "early release" track for sales-driven requests that don't
  fit the charter program.
- Vet recruits to ensure they're truly from your target market, not just easily
  excited early adopters.
- Brief every member that you're building a general product, not a custom
  build — and that this protects them (a one-off product can't sustain a
  supportable business).
- Release software to charter users before general release and confirm they're
  live and happy before launching publicly.
- For platform products, aim to end with six reference *applications* rather
  than six customers, and encourage your application partners to run their own
  charter user programs.
- If a corporate policy forbids talking to users, try hard to change it. If
  that fails, dust off your resume.

## Pitfalls
- Building a "special" by drifting toward one customer's specific needs.
- Letting sales use charter slots as a bargaining chip and overstuffing the
  program past what you can manage.
- Recruiting early adopters by accident — they're tolerant in ways your real
  market won't be, and you'll ship a product only early adopters want.
- Relying solely on filtered inputs (commissioned surveys, focus group reports,
  customer-service summaries) and skipping direct user contact.
- Settling for surface-level input ("I need to customize this page", "72% of
  our users said they want higher-resolution videos") instead of digging into
  underlying needs.

## Notable Frameworks / Definitions
- **Charter User Program** (also Customer Advisory Board, Customer Council,
  Voice of the Customer). A small set of target-market customers recruited at
  the start of a project to co-develop the product, with the explicit
  expectation that they become public reference customers at launch if they're
  satisfied.
- **The charter deal — customer benefits.** Early and significant product
  input; early access; significantly reduced cost, if any.
- **The charter deal — your benefits.** Ongoing dialog and access; access to
  their offices and users (or developers, for platform products); periodic
  group sessions at your office; prompt deployment of test versions and timely
  feedback; agreement to serve as public reference if happy with the delivered
  product.
