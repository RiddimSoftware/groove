# Chapter 38: Keys to Enterprise Products

## Premise
The enterprise software industry is in poor shape — most products are hard to
use, often don't really work, and demand enormous professional-services
spending to install, configure, and integrate. The structural cause is that the
buyer is not the user, so vendor incentives drift away from end-user
experience. The chapter lays out ten practices that separate good enterprise
products from the rest, plus a strict definition of what does and does not
qualify as a "product" — and specifically as a "solutions product."

## Key Principles
- The customer is not the user. The buyer signs the check; end-users in various
  roles, sysadmins, management, and adjacent business apps all depend on the
  product. Designing only for the buyer is how enterprise UX got this bad.
- Specials kill products. A customer offering a big check for seven specific
  features is the most dangerous offer in the business — take it and you become
  a custom-software shop with no general product, and the original customer
  will likely still be unhappy.
- Meet with many customers but never let them dictate requirements. Structure
  the intimacy through a Charter User Program: a half-dozen smart, motivated
  customers get early access in exchange for becoming reference customers.
- Channel design is product design. A product sold through SIs must not cut
  them out; a product sold through VARs must not overwhelm them. Provide value
  along every link in the chain.
- Installation, updates, and migration are first-class features. Consumer
  vendors know installs must be bulletproof; enterprise vendors too often
  assume a dedicated sysadmin will absorb whatever pain ships.
- Heavy configuration, customization, and integration burdens are mostly a
  symptom of poor product design. The professional-services industry surrounds
  enterprise software to fill that gap; Salesforce.com showed the bar can be
  raised.
- The internet has changed buying. Sales-rep charm still matters more than it
  should, but research and evaluation are online now — make the product easy
  to try and easy to buy.

## Practices
- Invest in interaction design, visual design, and usability testing — they
  are rare in enterprise and the absence shows.
- Ship a product that actually works without massive services billing to make
  it work.
- Run a Charter User Program; aim for six live, happy referenceable customers
  at general availability.
- Design explicitly for the sales channel you have — direct, SI, VAR, partner.
- Map every user population the product touches and design for each, not
  just the buyer.
- Treat install, upgrade, and migration as fully tested capabilities — the
  customer has a business to run; upgrades aren't your annual event.
- Reduce the configure/customize/integrate burden by improving the product
  itself.
- Support trial paths and online evaluation; make buying easy.

## Pitfalls
- Treating the buyer as the only "user."
- Saying yes to specials because the company needs the quarter.
- Confusing "listen to customers" with "let customers write your requirements."
- Letting the professional-services industry hide your product's defects.
- Marketing component products as "solutions" or "platforms" when they don't
  meet the bar — diluting both terms and confusing the market.

## Notable Frameworks / Definitions

**The top-10 enterprise practices:**
1. **Usability** — most enterprise apps are awful for daily users; raise the
   bar with interaction design, visual design, and usability testing.
2. **Product actually needs to work** — without massive services billing.
3. **Specials** — avoid the seven-features-for-a-big-check trap.
4. **Customers and Charter User Programs** — structure customer intimacy
   without ceding requirements; aim for six referenceable customers at release.
5. **Designing for the sales channel** — provide value to every link.
6. **The customer versus the user** — design for all user populations, not
   just the buyer.
7. **Product installation** — bulletproof, not days of sysadmin craft.
8. **Product configuration, customization, and integration** — treat the need
   for services as a product-design defect to reduce over time.
9. **Product update** — upgrades and migration are full product capabilities.
10. **The Sales Process** — buyers now evaluate online; make it easy to try
    and buy.

**What makes software a product (Cagan's bar):**
- People will pay for it; it delivers real and distinct value (typically with
  its own SKU). Payment may be tolerating advertising or paying for support
  rather than license fees.
- It works well in multiple customer installations — not a special, not custom
  software.
- Your field and/or channel can effectively sell it.
- Your company stands behind it with support and ongoing improvements.
- Customers and/or channel and/or services partners know how to install,
  configure, and customize it.

Software not yet successfully used by multiple customers is a wannabe-product,
not a product — the same way a platform with no apps on it isn't really a
platform.

**A solutions product** has all of the above, plus:
- Solves a business-level problem, often for specific industry verticals.
- May integrate one or more component products (yours or partners'),
  pre-integrated, and certified with partners' products where appropriate.

**A solutions product is NOT:**
- A set of instructions for using an existing product in a new way (customers
  won't pay for that).
- Field-built scripts or customizations (not supportable).

**Solutions marketing** (related but distinct) speaks to the business-level
problem, aligns with vertical industry needs, showcases live customer success
stories, and combines products with services and best practices to deliver
business results.
