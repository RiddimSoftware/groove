# Chapter 1: Key Roles and Responsibilities

## Premise
A modern software product team is staffed from a small set of distinct roles, each with a specific charter. Successful companies do not blur these roles together; they staff them deliberately and at the right ratios to keep engineering supplied with valuable, usable, feasible work.

## Key Principles
- Every product needs a single, accountable product manager whose job is (1) assessing opportunities and (2) defining the product to be built.
- Product definition describes *what* the product does and how it behaves — not how it will be implemented.
- Interaction design is its own competency, distinct from product management; it owns the deep understanding of target users, tasks, navigation, and flow.
- Project management is the execution discipline that takes a defined product through to release; it is not the same job as product management, though small teams sometimes share the person.
- Engineering builds the product for external customers and is deliberately distinguished from IT, which builds internal-use software.
- For Internet services, site operations is its own role — keeping the service running is too specialized to be a side responsibility of engineering.
- Role ratios matter: PMs, designers, and project managers exist to keep engineering supplied with valuable work, so understaffing them starves engineering.

## Practices
- **Product Manager.** Assess each opportunity (a lightweight *Opportunity Assessment* in place of a heavyweight MRD), then define the product in detail — features, functionality, UX, release criteria — preferably prototype-based rather than paper-based.
- **Interaction Designer.** Build deep understanding of each persona and design tasks, navigation, and flow that are both usable and valuable. Work in tight collaboration with the PM.
- **Project Manager.** Own scheduling and tracking. May be a dedicated PjM, the engineering manager, or (on smaller efforts) the PM — but larger projects benefit from a dedicated, skilled project manager.
- **Engineering.** Build and maintain the product for external customers. Do not conflate with IT.
- **Site Operations.** For Internet services, run the production service as a first-class competency.
- **Product Marketing.** Tell the world about the product — positioning, launch, sales-channel enablement, online and influencer marketing programs.
- **On Agile/Scrum teams.** The product manager typically serves as the Product Owner and the project manager typically serves as the ScrumMaster; other roles are unchanged.

## Pitfalls
- Letting marketing-titled people own product definition without the skills to discover something valuable, usable, and feasible.
- Combining the product manager and product marketing roles into one job — the skills are different and bandwidth rarely allows both to be done well.
- Failing to draw a clear line between engineering (external product) and IT (internal apps).
- Microsoft's nomenclature trap: calling the product-definition role "program manager" because "product manager" is already used for the marketing function — even when the people are doing real product management, the titles confuse the rest of the industry.
- Under-staffing PMs and designers and then wondering why engineering output is low-value.

## Notable Frameworks / Definitions
- **Opportunity Assessment.** Cagan's lighter-weight alternative to a Market Requirements Document (MRD), used to decide whether an idea is worth pursuing.
- **Product Spec / PRD.** The detailed definition of the product to be built; Cagan advocates a prototype-based version rather than a paper PRD, and it must describe behavior, not implementation.
- **Recommended role ratios.**
  - ~1 product manager per 5–10 engineers.
  - 1 interaction designer can support ~2 product managers.
  - 1 visual designer can support ~4 interaction designers.
  - Dedicated project managers for projects larger than ~5 engineers, and one per release train when using the train model.
- **Train model of releases.** Ship on a regular cadence (every one to four weeks); features that aren't ready ride the next train. Requires dedicated project managers tied to releases rather than projects.
