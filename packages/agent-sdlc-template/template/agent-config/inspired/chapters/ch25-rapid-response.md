# Chapter 25: Rapid Response

## Premise
Launch is not the finish line; it is the moment your opportunity to learn and correct is greatest. Most orgs disband the launch team and reassign people just when the highest-ROI work is sitting right in front of them. Slightly extending every project to include a deliberate post-launch *rapid response* phase — typically a few days to a week — produces returns that no other phase of the process can match.

## Key Principles
- Treat product launch as a phase, not a finish line. Don't confuse launching with success.
- Resources marshaled for launch evaporate too quickly in most organizations. This is a project-management and product-development process failing, not a resourcing reality.
- Extending the project to cover rapid response is not a hard sell to management — no phase of the process will provide a better ROI.
- Even with great early prototyping and validation and a strong QA team, expect issues that can only be discovered once you're live. The question is not whether there will be issues — it's how quickly you'll address them.
- The traditional approach (wait, see, scope a follow-on point release on the original release cadence) takes far too long and costs far too much.
- This pattern was born in consumer Internet services where it's especially critical, but it applies to platform, infrastructure, and enterprise products too.

## Practices
- **Schedule the rapid-response phase explicitly** as part of every project — typically a few days to a week beginning at launch.
- **Have a clear, prioritized set of business metrics in place before launch.** Page views, registered users, time on site, conversion, subscriptions, ad revenue — the right set depends on the product. Know what counts as success and what counts as a problem.
- **Instrument and watch.** Consumer Internet services have it easy — services like Google Analytics make near-real-time usage data accessible. Cagan reports looking at his sites' analytics nearly daily: traffic sources, favorite pages and activities, session length, page depth, return frequency.
- **For enterprise software, send members of the product team — PM, engineers, designers — to the customer site for installation and go-live.** Issues are identified and resolved dramatically faster when a team member knows they're staying on-site until the customer is live and referenceable.
- **Convene the product team daily, at minimum, during rapid response.** PM, interaction designer, lead engineers, customer service, marketing — prioritize identified issues and decide the best response.
- **Be ready to ship a hot fix or post explanatory content quickly.** Substantial improvements to effectiveness are achievable in a very short window if the team is prepared and understands the urgency.
- **Don't rely on analytics alone.** Use surveys, email discussions, message boards, field testing. Customer service, sales, and partners are also good sources of input for rapid-response fixes.

## Pitfalls
- Reallocating the launch team to the next project the moment the product ships.
- Treating post-launch issues as something to bundle into the next regularly scheduled point release.
- Assuming pre-launch validation and a strong QA pass mean nothing significant will surface live.
- Watching only analytics; ignoring qualitative signals from users, support, and the field.
- For enterprise: shipping the install and stepping back, instead of staying on-site through go-live.

## Notable Frameworks / Definitions
- **Rapid response.** A scheduled, named project phase that begins at launch and runs typically a few days to a week. Its purpose is to respond quickly to what's learned once the product is live — identify issues, prioritize daily, and ship hot fixes or content updates fast.
- **Prerequisites for rapid response.** A clear, prioritized set of business metrics; live instrumentation; daily team cadence; on-site presence for enterprise go-lives; and an explicit understanding that finishing what you launched can save entire release cycles.
