# Chapter 34: Fear, Greed and Lust

## Premise
People buy and use products for emotional reasons, not for rational
feature-by-feature comparisons. Product people from scientific and business
backgrounds tend to miss this, but the strongest products are explicitly
designed to speak to a dominant emotion in the target buyer. The real
competition is whoever else can meet that emotional need — often an offline
alternative, not the obvious online rival.

## Key Principles
- Buying and use decisions are primarily emotional. The best marketers know
  this; the best product people make sure their products speak to the emotions.
- In the enterprise space, the dominant emotions are usually fear or greed
  (competitor will beat me, hackers will get in, customers will leave; or I'll
  make more money, save money, or stop overspending).
- In the consumer space, emotions are more personal: loneliness (make friends),
  love or lust (find a date), greed (win money), pride (show off photos or
  taste).
- Different user types bring different emotional needs. An eBay power seller, a
  bargain hunter, and a buyer chasing the thrill of competition are not the same
  user even though they share a site.
- Once you know the dominant emotion, your real competition is whatever else can
  meet that need — often an offline alternative, not the obvious online rival.

## Practices
- Apply the "emotional lens" to your product: ask what your visual design says
  about the emotion, which features speak to it, and which features get in the
  way of speaking to it.
- During prototype testing with target users, after confirming they can figure
  out how to use the product, use the session as a one-on-one focus group to
  identify which emotion is driving them and how well the product meets it.
- Identify and prioritize the dominant buying emotions for your customers, then
  audit every feature and visual element against that emotion.
- Treat interaction design, visual design, and usability testing as central to
  winning products — they are the channels through which the product speaks to
  emotion.

## Pitfalls
- Treating products as purely rational artifacts to be sold on feature lists.
- Scoping competition only to companies in your obvious category — ignoring the
  offline or adjacent alternatives where the customer's emotional need can be
  met another way.
- Assuming a single "user" exists for your product when in fact several
  different users with different emotional drivers share it.
