# Chapter 40: Best Practices Summary

## Premise
From more than 20 years of practice, Cagan distills the ten techniques he
considers most important for creating successful and inspiring products. Each
is treated in detail elsewhere in the book; this chapter is the curated
top-10 list.

## Key Principles
- Product management is a distinct role from product marketing and project
  management — and the most common cause of weak product organizations is
  product leaders spending their time on those other activities instead.
- User experience is not a polish layer; for most software products, it is
  the product. Creating it requires close collaboration between an interaction
  designer, an engineer, and the PM.
- The PM's primary job is discovery — proving that a product is valuable,
  usable, and feasible — before building it.
- Decisions, not features, are the unit of product management. Product
  principles, personas, and opportunity assessments exist to help make the
  hard choices well.
- Improvement comes from measuring actual product use, not from adding
  whatever features customers request.

## Notable Frameworks / Definitions
The book's top-10 best practices, in Cagan's order and framing:

1. **The role of product management.** Too many product leaders spend their
   time on product marketing or project management. These activities are not
   a substitute for true product management.
2. **The role of user experience.** For most software products, the user
   experience is all-important. Devising a good UX requires close
   collaboration with an interaction designer and an engineer to produce
   something that is valuable, usable, and feasible.
3. **Opportunity assessments.** Lightweight, to-the-point assessments that
   replace the old "MRD" (market requirements document). Before jumping into
   the solution, confirm what problem you're solving, who you're solving it
   for, and how you'll know if you succeeded.
4. **Charter user program.** It is shocking how many product teams think they
   can build great products without talking to users and customers. Doing
   this one thing right forces several other things to go right, starting
   with direct and intense user interaction.
5. **Product principles.** Product management is all about making choices.
   Product principles help you identify and prioritize what you believe is
   important.
6. **Personas.** Personas focus a release and surface the key behaviors and
   underlying emotions of the target users — another tool for making the
   difficult choices an inspiring product demands.
7. **Focus on discovery.** The PM's primary responsibility is to discover a
   product that is valuable, usable, and feasible. It makes no sense to
   proceed to building until you have evidence you've discovered that
   product.
8. **The use of prototypes.** Building a high-fidelity prototype forces deeper
   thinking about the solution, lets you test ideas with real users, and is
   the most useful way to describe the product to the rest of the team.
9. **Test prototype with target users.** Knowing how to get feedback on
   product ideas is probably the single most important skill for product
   managers. Master the prototype-testing techniques.
10. **Measure to improve.** Successful product managers use data to make
    important decisions, especially when improving an existing product.
    Improving a product is not about adding features customers request; it
    is about analyzing actual use and relentlessly driving the key metrics.
