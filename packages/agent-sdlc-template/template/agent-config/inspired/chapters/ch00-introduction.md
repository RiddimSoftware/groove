# Introduction

## Premise
A technically excellent engineering effort is wasted if the team is not given something worthwhile to build. Most companies are still bad at product management — the discipline of discovering a product that is *valuable, usable, and feasible* — and that gap between the state of the art and the state of the practice is what this book exists to close.

## Key Principles
- It does not matter how good the engineering team is if they are not given something worthwhile to build.
- Engineering a product well is not enough; you also have to discover one that is valuable, usable, and feasible.
- Behind every inspiring product there is someone explicitly accountable for product definition.
- Most products are not inspiring, and life is too short for bad products — the failure rate is a process problem, not a talent problem.
- The "best companies" produce products in a fundamentally different way from "most companies"; the methods can be learned.
- Academia and most industry bodies are stuck in the failed models of the past; the playbook has to come from inside successful product orgs.

## Practices
- Treat product work as three distinct concerns and staff/process each one deliberately:
  - **People** — the product organization: who is on the team and what each role is responsible for.
  - **Process** — the activities and best practices used to repeatedly discover and build successful products.
  - **Product** — the defining characteristics of products that customers actually love.
- Read topics independently. Each chapter is self-contained; the book is a reference, not a linear argument.
- Pair the book with the live examples (opportunity assessments, product principles, strategies, roadmaps, specs, prototypes, personas, prototype-testing tasks) maintained on the Silicon Valley Product Group site, because product examples date faster than the underlying ideas.

## Pitfalls
- Confusing "the reviewers loved it" or "we shipped on time" with success — the only test is whether customers wanted it.
- Believing a strong engineering team alone is sufficient; HP's AI workstation story is the cautionary tale Cagan opens the book with.
- Assuming product management is a marketing sub-function or a documentation job, rather than the discipline of discovering the right product.
- Treating product discovery as something that can be piecemealed after the fact: once the minimal successful product is found, you cannot break it apart and expect the same result.

## Notable Frameworks / Definitions
- **Valuable, Usable, Feasible.** The product manager's job is to discover a product that is all three.
- **The three-part structure of the book — People, Process, Product.** Everything starts with the people; process is what lets those people produce inspiring products repeatedly; product is what they end up shipping.
- **Cagan's foundational truths about great products** (paraphrased from the introduction's list):
  1. The product manager's job is to discover a product that is valuable, usable, and feasible.
  2. Product discovery is a collaboration between the product manager, interaction designer, and software architect.
  3. Engineering is important and difficult, but user experience design is even more important — and usually more difficult.
  4. Engineers are typically poor at UX design: engineers think in implementation models, users think in conceptual models.
  5. User experience design means both interaction design and visual design (and industrial design for hardware).
  6. Functionality (requirements) and user experience design are inherently intertwined.
  7. Product ideas must be tested — early and often — on actual target users to arrive at something valuable and usable.
  8. A high-fidelity prototype is required so that ideas can be tested quickly, easily, and frequently on real users with a realistic experience.
  9. The product manager's job is to identify the *minimal* possible product that meets the valuable/usable/feasible bar, minimizing time to market and user complexity.
  10. Once that minimal successful product has been discovered and validated, it cannot be piecemealed and still produce the same results.
- **Audience.** Software product teams — especially Internet software teams, both consumer and business — and specifically whoever is accountable for defining the product, regardless of title (PM, founder, executive, lead engineer, designer). Not intended for non-software products or custom-software projects.
