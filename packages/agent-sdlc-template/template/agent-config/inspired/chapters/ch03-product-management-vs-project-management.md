# Chapter 3: Product Management vs. Project Management

## Premise
Product management (discovering a product that is valuable, usable, and feasible) and project management (executing to deliver that product) are different jobs. The shipped-software world could often combine them in one person; the Internet world, with its train-model release cadences and parallel development, cannot. Internet companies that don't separate the roles thrash on releases and ship late.

## Key Principles
- Product management is about discovering the right product to build; project management is about getting that product out the door. Different skills, different mindsets.
- The Internet's smaller, more frequent releases against a shared code base force parallel development, which forces the train model, which forces dedicated release-level project management.
- A release train cuts across multiple projects and multiple product managers — project management for a train belongs to the *release*, not to any one project or PM.
- Even for shipped software, the two roles are usefully separate; great PMs can pick up strong PjM skills, but the responsibilities still want different owners.
- A strong project management discipline is often the difference between a product that ships and a product that gets stuck in perpetual delay.

## Practices
- For Internet services on a train model, staff project managers dedicated to the release train (a "train conductor"), not to individual projects.
- Coordinate release-level work across release management, engineering, site operations, customer service, and product management through that release-train project manager.
- Encourage PMs to develop project management skills — at minimum it gets the product to market faster — while still keeping the roles organizationally distinct.
- Don't equate project management with Microsoft Project. The real job is framing, decisiveness, and removing obstacles.

## Pitfalls
- Importing shipped-software habits — one PM doubling as project manager — into Internet services with train-model releases. The result is consistent release delays and longer cycle times than necessary.
- Treating a release as if it belonged to a single product manager when the train actually contains features from many projects and PMs.
- Reducing project management to Gantt charts and ignoring the framing, judgment, and decisiveness work that actually moves a release forward.

## Notable Frameworks / Definitions
- **The Train Model of Releases.** Ship on a regular cadence; a train carries features from many projects, and any feature not ready simply moves to the next train. Requires strong, dedicated project management at the release level. Used widely by Internet companies past the startup stage.
- **Seven characteristics of great project managers** (drawn from Cagan's portrait of Lynn Reedy at eBay):
  1. **Sense of urgency.** They convey it on entry; meetings get down to business fast.
  2. **Framers.** They identify and frame the problem precisely so meetings are constructive.
  3. **Clear thinking.** They untangle politics, agendas, and emotion from the underlying issue and re-focus the team on the solution.
  4. **Data driven.** They insist on measurement and resist shoot-from-the-hip decisions under time pressure.
  5. **Decisiveness.** They drive decisions through people who don't report to them — using urgency, framing, transparent reasoning, and data — and they know when to escalate.
  6. **Judgment.** They know when to push, when to escalate, when to gather more information, and when to take someone aside privately.
  7. **Attitude.** They treat the long list of valid reasons a product can't ship as obstacles to be removed. Great project managers are great problem solvers; they don't make excuses, they make it happen.
