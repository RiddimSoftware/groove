# Chapter 16: Market Research

## Premise
Market research tools have gotten dramatically better, and product teams should
use them aggressively to refine existing products and understand users. But
none of these tools will tell you what product to build. Winning products come
from the deep understanding of user needs combined with an equally deep
understanding of what's now possible — not from research.

## Key Principles
- Market research is input to the product creation process, not the steering
  wheel for it. Teams get into trouble when they try to steer with research.
- No winning product was created by market research — not Google, eBay, the
  iPod or iPhone, FaceBook, or MySpace.
- If you ask customers what they want, you'll get incremental improvements to
  what they already have, at best — more often a random collection of band-aid
  features, not the dramatically better solution you're looking for.
- The right questions for the product discovery process are different: What
  technologies can I apply to solve this problem in a better way? What should
  the user experience be?
- Once you have a launched product and active customers, research becomes
  genuinely useful for *refining* it — distinct from *conceiving* a new one.
- Customers don't know what's possible (they lack knowledge of the enabling
  technologies) and they don't know what they want until they see it. Both
  limits apply doubly to focus groups.

## Practices
- Run customer surveys — they're cheap and powerful, especially with conjoint
  analysis. Invest care in writing the questions; biased or unclear questions
  let your team dismiss the data as "garbage in, garbage out." Set the
  expectation internally that survey data is one input, not the answer.
- Instrument your product (web or otherwise) for analytics early and watch it
  continuously. For non-web products, send aggregated, non-personally
  identifiable usage data home.
- Mine the data you already have. Modern tools can answer thousands of
  questions across analytics, billing, account, and product data quickly.
- Do site visits. They are expensive and time-consuming, but each one surfaces
  something you wouldn't have learned any other way. Pick them carefully.
- Use personas, especially for product definition and design (see Chapter 17).
- Run usability tests early and often, on prototypes and on shipped products.
  Watching users gauges enthusiasm and frustration, and shows actions, not just
  words.
- Take competitors seriously — every product has at least some things it does
  well. Learn from their successes and mistakes.
- If you must run focus groups, the product manager attends every session in
  person. Logistics and facilitation can be delegated; interpretation and
  analysis cannot.

## Pitfalls
- Depending on research tools to answer questions they're inherently incapable
  of answering — like "what should we build next?"
- Writing off competitors as clueless instead of mining what they do well.
- Running focus groups where attendees influence each other, the most
  articulate dominate, and you walk away thinking you have customer input.
- Using focus groups before customers can actually see and interact with the
  product — at which point they can't give you useful data about it anyway.
- Letting focus groups be used for political cover rather than understanding.

## Notable Frameworks / Definitions
- **Capabilities (what research can answer).** Who your users really are; how
  they use your product; whether they can figure it out and where they
  stumble; why they use it; what they like; what they want added or changed.
- **Limitations (what research cannot answer).** What product to build; what
  technologies can solve the problem in a better way; what the user experience
  should be.
- **Market research toolkit.** Customer surveys (with conjoint analysis), site
  analytics, data mining, site visits, personas, usability testing, competitive
  analysis. Focus groups, deliberately omitted from the main list, are
  treated separately and skeptically.
- **Why focus groups fail at discovering products.** (1) Customers don't know
  what's possible — they lack visibility into the enabling technologies. (2)
  Customers don't know what they want — it's very hard to envision a solution
  without seeing it.
