# Chapter 7: Managing Product Managers

## Premise
The Director / VP of Product Management has two responsibilities: build a team
of strong product managers, and own the company's overall product strategy and
portfolio. Few roles will more directly determine whether the company succeeds
or sinks, and the outcomes tend to be either massive success or massive failure
with little in between.

## Key Principles
- Building and developing the team is the manager's primary job — uniquely
  critical here because each product has only one PM, so a weak PM cannot be
  carried by teammates.
- Every new PM — even experienced ones — needs roughly three months of hard
  learning (users, technology, market, competition) before being trusted to
  guide a product.
- Hire people smarter than yourself, then get out of their way. The best way to
  look good is to let your team look good.
- Empower only after you have verified capability. Empowering people who aren't
  capable is abdication; micromanaging people who aren't capable means you're
  doing their job for them.
- The head of product owns the product strategy, the product vision, the
  product principles, and the cross-product portfolio roadmap — and must keep
  these aligned to the business strategy.
- The head of product must have a seat at the executive table. Burying product
  inside marketing or engineering distorts what gets built.
- Specials — explicitly-defined custom work traded for a customer's commitment
  to buy — are "bad revenue." They damage the customer experience and the
  product's ability to grow.

## Practices
- Stand up a structured three-month immersion program (users, technology,
  market) for every new PM and for in-flight PMs who skipped it.
- Resolve cross-product conflicts personally — even great PMs will optimize
  their own product against others'.
- Manage executive relationships actively. The CEO and other key stakeholders
  must trust the head of product; be transparent, accessible, and willing to
  push back on vacillating priorities.
- If a PM is not up to the job and won't get there, move them out promptly.
  Help them land somewhere they can succeed, but don't let the team carry them.
- Track Net Promoter Score (NPS) as the primary product-success signal — it
  keeps the focus on creating happy customers, which fuels organic growth and
  lowers sales and marketing cost.
- When a centralized engineering org and decentralized business units coexist,
  integrate product management and design into the business units so the BU
  owners have product authority over their own line.

## Pitfalls
- Inheriting "product managers" who are really product marketers and treating
  the title as if it solves the problem.
- Putting product management inside marketing — rests on the misconception that
  products come from asking customers — and combining product management with
  product marketing, which guarantees one or both gets executed poorly.
- Putting product management inside engineering — engineering optimizes for
  "building the product right" rather than "building the right product," which
  squeezes out discovery.
- Micromanaging strong PMs; they will stop taking ownership.
- Letting good short-term revenue from sponsorships or partnerships erode the
  customer experience — the NPS will surface the cost, but only after the damage.

## Notable Frameworks / Definitions
- **Two responsibilities of the head of product:** (1) build a strong team of
  product managers; (2) own the company's product strategy and portfolio.
- **Net Promoter Score (NPS):** Ask customers, on a 0-10 scale, how likely they
  are to recommend the product. Promoters are 9-10, passives are 7-8,
  detractors are 0-6. NPS = % promoters - % detractors.
- **Good revenue vs. bad revenue:** Revenue that preserves or improves the
  customer experience (and NPS) is good; revenue that degrades it — especially
  "specials" — is bad regardless of the dollar amount.
- **Where product management should live:** Ideally its own organization on par
  with engineering and marketing — typically including design — under a VP of
  Product or Chief Product Officer.
