# Chapter 6: Recruiting Product Managers

## Premise
Great product managers are rare but recognizable: they share a specific set of
personal traits that cannot be taught, plus a set of skills that can. Hire for the
traits, develop the skills, and look for these people anywhere in the
organization — not just marketing or business school.

## Key Principles
- Most skills can be learned; the foundational traits cannot. Hire on traits first.
- A's hire A's; B's hire C's. Insecure hiring managers shrink the team's ceiling.
- Domain expertise is overrated; a strong PM comes up to speed on a new domain in
  one to three months. Deep domain mastery can be a liability because it tempts the
  PM to "speak for the target customer."
- About 80% of PM skills transfer across product types (enterprise, infrastructure,
  consumer, hardware). The other 20% is what's worth interviewing for.
- The PM "is the CEO of the product" — takes the blame, gives away the credit,
  and accepts that obstacles to ship are theirs to overcome.
- The PM influences without authority, so integrity is operational, not decorative:
  trust and respect are how the work actually gets done.
- Outstanding product leaders exist across every age, gender, culture, and
  background. Hiring biases — including a bias toward native English speakers —
  cause companies to miss the strongest candidates.

## Practices
- Look inside the company first. Strong PMs are often hiding as engineers, UX
  designers, SEs, customer service, professional services, product marketing, or
  sales — and frequently surface themselves by asking how to get more involved
  with the product.
- Probe product passion by asking candidates about favorite products (across
  domains), what they would improve, and about bad products. Insincere passion
  is audible.
- Use Microsoft-style problem-solving interviews: don't test what they know,
  test how they reason when they don't know. Push past their first answer until
  they hit a scenario they can't solve, then ask how they'd attack it.
- Have two or three of the smartest people in the company interview the candidate
  for raw intelligence.
- Require written work samples (white papers, strategy docs) — speaking can be
  judged in the interview, writing must be assessed deliberately.
- For experienced PMs, press for specifics on how they handled stress, late
  asks, and big-customer special requests.
- Be frank with candidates about the level of effort and ownership the role
  demands. If you ever have to tell a PM to come in during a critical moment,
  you have the wrong person.

## Pitfalls
- Hiring an MBA and assuming they know product management. Top-tier MBA programs
  almost never teach it.
- Treating "talks to customers" as the PM job description and stocking the role
  out of marketing.
- Confusing knowledge with intelligence in interviews — letting a candidate
  recite domain facts instead of reasoning through unfamiliar problems.
- Filtering candidates by accent, age, gender, or pedigree instead of by traits.
  The 22-year-old's idea might be the next Facebook.
- Letting PMs burn 70-hour weeks while doing the wrong work — confusing urgent
  with important and never producing forward motion on the product.
- Believing deep domain experts can stand in for users; long tenure in a domain
  often hardens the assumption that the PM is the target customer.

## Notable Frameworks / Definitions
- **Personal traits (foundational, hard to teach):** Product Passion, Customer
  Empathy, Intelligence, Work Ethic, Integrity, Confidence, Attitude (CEO of the
  product).
- **Skills (learnable):** Applying Technology, Focus, Time Management,
  Communication Skills (writing and presenting), Business Skills (the PM must be
  "bilingual" — fluent with engineers about technology and with executives about
  cost structures, margins, market share, positioning, and brand).
- **Domain rule of thumb:** ~80% of a PM's talents transfer across product
  types; expect one to three months to come up to speed on a new domain.
