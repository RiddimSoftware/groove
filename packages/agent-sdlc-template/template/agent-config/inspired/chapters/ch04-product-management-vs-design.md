# Chapter 4: Product Management vs. Design

## Premise
A good product requires a good user experience, and a good user experience requires close collaboration between product management and a real user-experience design competency. Most companies either don't staff UX at all — letting UI engineers or PMs improvise the design — or bolt on visual design as decorative veneer at the end. That is a serious problem, and the design community has largely failed to evangelize itself to the rest of the product team.

## Key Principles
- User experience design is a distinct competency from product management; the two roles must collaborate from the start, not hand off at the end.
- "Design" is plural: interaction design, visual design, rapid prototyping, and usability testing are four different roles, even when one person covers more than one.
- Visual design isn't decoration — it communicates and evokes emotion in the product, which matters more than most teams think.
- The product manager, interaction designer, and software architect together discover the blend of requirements and design that is valuable, usable, *and* feasible.
- For enterprise products, where competitors typically have weak UX, investing in user experience is one of the easiest ways to differentiate.

## Practices
- Staff the four UX roles, doubling them up only when team size genuinely demands it (e.g., a small team of three: PM + interaction designer who also runs usability tests + visual designer who also prototypes).
- Have the interaction designer map product requirements to a design represented by wireframes, then hand those to the visual designer to put the flesh on the wireframe.
- Use rapid prototyping to capture the PM's and designers' ideas in something testable and iterable on real users.
- Run usability testing as its own discipline: recruit appropriate subjects, administer tests, evaluate results, recommend alternatives.
- Pull a software architect in alongside the designers to review progress and prototypes so the design stays feasible.
- For rapid prototyping, borrow a developer from engineering — but make it explicit the prototype code is throw-away and not eligible for reuse in the real product.

## Pitfalls
- Letting UI engineers default-design the product because no one else is responsible for UX.
- Bolting visual design onto an otherwise-finished product, just before QA, as cosmetic veneer.
- Believing "we value design" without understanding which roles are needed or how good design actually comes about.
- The design community preaching only to itself instead of educating the wider product team — especially PMs — about what design contributes.
- Outsourcing the interaction designer role. Three specific reasons it fails:
  1. It takes multiple projects to build the deep understanding of users and customers; design contracts rarely span that long, and the knowledge is lost when the contract ends.
  2. The interaction designer must be on hand and deeply involved from kickoff to launch to answer the hundreds of detailed questions that arise during build and test.
  3. UX is too core to the company to live outside it — if you have to outsource something, outsource QA before you outsource interaction design.

## Notable Frameworks / Definitions
- **The four UX design roles essential to a good user experience:**
  1. **Interaction Design.** Develop deep understanding of target users; design tasks, navigation, and flow that are valuable and usable; produce wireframes.
  2. **Visual Design.** Put flesh on the wireframe — layout, color, typography — and use the visual language to evoke emotion in the product.
  3. **Rapid Prototyping.** Capture PM and designer ideas as a prototype that can be tested on real users and iterated.
  4. **Usability Testing.** Research and analyze users; evaluate whether products or prototypes let users easily achieve objectives; recruit subjects; recommend alternatives.
- **What you can vs. cannot outsource.** Visual design — yes, especially with a strong in-house interaction designer. User research / usability testing — yes, though informal in-house testing is often better and cheaper. Rapid prototyping — yes, using a borrowed engineer treating the code as throw-away. Interaction design — no.
