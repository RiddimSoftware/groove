# Chapter 2: Product Management vs. Product Marketing

## Premise
Roughly nine in ten product releases fail to meet their objectives, and the root cause is usually a poorly defined product manager role rather than bad engineering. Product management (defining the product) and product marketing (telling the world about it) are fundamentally different jobs, and conflating them — or splitting product management across two people — wastes entire release cycles.

## Key Principles
- Every product needs a single, accountable product manager who owns the product definition: the combination of product requirements and user experience design that describes the product to be built.
- Product management is about *creating* the product; product marketing is about *telling the world* about it. Both are critical, but they require different skills.
- High-level requirements cannot be defined independently of the detailed requirements and the user experience — splitting "business" and "technical" PMs is based on a flawed view of software.
- Behind every great product is a single individual responsible for that product's definition, regardless of title or org chart.
- It doesn't matter how great your engineering organization is if the product manager doesn't give engineers something valuable, usable, and feasible to build.

## Practices
- Clearly define and staff the two roles separately in your org:
  - **Product Manager:** define the product in detail, validate it with real customers and users.
  - **Product Marketing:** positioning, messaging, pricing, launch management, sales-channel tools, online and influencer marketing programs.
- Establish two main interactions between them: product marketing is a key input to product requirements (owned by the PM), and the PM is a key input to marketing messages (owned by product marketing).
- For all but the simplest products, treat product management as an all-consuming, full-time job — staff it that way.

## Pitfalls
- **Marketing-Driven Product.** A marketing-titled "PM" hands market requirements to engineering and bypasses detailed product definition (and often UX entirely). The team treats them as a sales-enablement and data-sheet resource; real product work falls on whoever has the bandwidth — a lead engineer, an interaction designer, or a manager. The product is in trouble from the start.
- **Two People, One Role.** Splitting product management into a "business product manager" (high-level requirements) and a "technical product manager" / "product owner" (low-level requirements). No one truly owns the product or feels ultimately responsible for it. The PM becomes a spec-generation service. Common at large multi-BU companies and a frequent cause of those companies losing their ability to ship inspiring products.
- **One Person, Two Roles.** Asking one person to do both product management *and* product marketing. People who can do both well are very rare, the roles don't scale combined, and bandwidth is the binding constraint even when skill exists. Especially toxic at enterprise software companies, where sales-force support crowds out discovery and the PM becomes a conduit passing perceived requirements from big customers through sales reps into engineering.
- Mistaking respect for product marketing for an argument to merge the roles — product marketing matters and is valuable, but it has very little to do with defining a product.

## Notable Frameworks / Definitions
- **Three failure modes for the PM role** Cagan repeatedly encounters:
  1. **Marketing-Driven Product** — a marketing-titled PM produces market requirements, then engineering builds with no detailed product definition or UX.
  2. **Two People, One Role** — product definition split between a "business" PM and a "technical" PM; no single owner.
  3. **One Person, Two Roles** — one person covers both product management and product marketing; both jobs suffer.
- **Product Manager (Cagan's definition).** The person responsible for defining — in detail — the product to be built, and validating that product with real customers and users.
- **Product Marketing (Cagan's definition).** The person responsible for telling the world about the product: positioning, messaging, pricing, launch, sales-channel enablement, and lead programs such as online and influencer marketing.
