# Chapter 11: Assessing Product Opportunities

## Premise
Before committing engineering effort to a product, the product manager owes the
company a quick, lightweight, written assessment of the opportunity — the problem
to be solved, not the solution. Heavyweight MRDs go unread and intuition-driven
go-decisions burn cycles; a short Opportunity Assessment either kills a bad idea
early or sharpens a good one before investment.

## Key Principles
- The Opportunity Assessment evaluates the problem, not the solution. Naming a
  solution too early causes teams to throw out the opportunity when their first
  solution stumbles.
- Choosing the right set of products to pursue is among the most important
  decisions a company makes — bad ideas must be killed at this stage, before
  significant time and money is spent.
- New product work and improvement of existing products are both "product
  investments." Don't preallocate by ratio; pick the best opportunities.
- The biggest bang for the buck is often improving an underperforming existing
  product (e.g. lifting a 9% subscription conversion to 18%), not building a new
  one.
- The hardest question to answer is usually the first — a crisp value proposition.
  Most product managers respond with a rambling feature list instead.
- Even when a CEO mandates a product, run the assessment anyway; you may change
  the CEO's mind, and at minimum you'll understand what you're committing to.

## Practices
- Answer the 10 Opportunity Assessment questions for every new product effort and
  present the result to senior management for an explicit go/no-go.
- Be conservative when sizing the market; not every opportunity needs to be a
  billion-dollar one. Use industry analysts, trade associations, finance, and
  bottom-up math.
- Spell out the go-to-market strategy early — it materially shapes product
  requirements (e.g. selling through system integrators imposes extensibility
  needs).
- Capture special "solution requirements" (dependencies, partner, branding
  constraints) without designing the product.
- Make a friend in finance. Use them to understand product economics (revenue
  model, CAC, LTV, ROI), surface customer information you don't otherwise see, and
  co-author the business case.

## Pitfalls
- Treating the MRD as a product spec, writing it long, and having no one read it.
- Letting a single large customer fund a "special" and treating that as the basis
  for a product.
- Conflating problem and solution, then "throwing the baby out with the bathwater"
  when the chosen solution doesn't work.
- Assuming a weak existing product is "as good as it can be" and that yet another
  new product is easier than fixing the one you have — under-investment in design
  and UX recreates the same problem.
- Product managers (especially ex-engineers) having only a shallow grasp of how
  their product makes money.

## Notable Frameworks / Definitions
The 10 Opportunity Assessment questions, in the book's framing:

1. Exactly what problem will this solve? (value proposition)
2. For whom do we solve that problem? (target market)
3. How big is the opportunity? (market size)
4. How will we measure success? (metrics/revenue strategy)
5. What alternatives are out there now? (competitive landscape)
6. Why are we best suited to pursue this? (our differentiator)
7. Why now? (market window)
8. How will we get this product to market? (go-to-market strategy)
9. What factors are critical to success? (solution requirements)
10. Given the above, what's the recommendation? (go or no-go)

None of the questions speaks to the actual solution — by design.
