# Chapter 9: Deputy Product Managers

## Premise
Product people are in the idea business, and the supply of great ideas is
limited by the set of smart minds the PM actually consults. The single most
important habit Cagan learned is to seek out the smartest people in the
company — wherever they're hiding — and treat them as "deputy product
managers" whose insight feeds the product.

## Key Principles
- Every organization has a few very smart people; some are hidden by office
  politics, ego, xenophobia, or insecure managers. Finding them is the PM's job.
- Smart minds turn up in engineering, sales, customer service, professional
  services, SE ranks, the exec team, the board, and among founders — not just
  on the product team.
- Field people (SEs, customer-facing roles) often have great insight into
  customer needs; engineers often have great insight into available
  technologies. The strongest deputies combine both.
- Cultural and demographic biases hide product minds in plain sight: youth,
  introversion, gender, nationality, accent. Don't let those signals filter
  them out.
- Founders with strong product instincts are usually willing to be a sounding
  board if you open the channel. Use them.
- The blocker to doing all this is the PM's ego — the belief that the PM must
  personally originate every big idea. The only thing that matters is shipping
  great products; where the idea came from is not the point.

## Practices
Cagan's techniques for finding deputy product managers:
- **Ask.** Ask people at all levels of the company who the really great minds
  are. The answers will surprise you.
- **MBWA — Management By Wandering Around.** Leave the office or cube and
  spend informal time with people across the company. From the HP Way.
- **Listen** — really listen — to the dialog in meetings and conversations.
- **Keep your door open** so anyone can drop in with a product suggestion.
- **Share** the problems you're struggling with; word gets around and
  suggestions arrive.
- **Hang out** with people at all levels — not just other PMs or other execs.
- When you find a deputy, give them public recognition as such, recruit them
  onto the product team when possible, and use them as a sounding board on
  major product decisions.

## Pitfalls
- Trusting a manager's assessment of their own report without verifying — an
  insecure manager will actively bad-mouth the smartest person on their team.
- Underutilizing people because they're young, quiet, introverted, female, or
  from a culture less inclined to challenge authority.
- Assuming a great engineer's full potential is engineering. Some have an
  equally strong product mind and become company-wide thought leaders even if
  they never formally switch roles.
- Treating accessible founders as off-limits or "too senior" — they're often
  trying not to micromanage, but are happy to give product feedback when asked.
- Letting ego decide whose ideas count. The PM's job is to recognize and ship
  great ideas, not to be their sole author.
