# Chapter 28: Startup Product Management

## Premise
The default startup playbook — raise seed, hire engineers, build in stealth, show the result months later — is expensive, slow, and most often fatal because the engineering team is asked to construct a product whose requirements and design are still being figured out on the fly. The right first hires are a product manager, an interaction designer, and a prototyper; their job is to run rapid product discovery so that when engineering arrives, the spec is already validated.

## Key Principles
- A startup is fundamentally a new-product-creation engine — the first job is to figure out *what* to build, not to start building.
- Hiring engineers before you have a validated product idea burns the seed round on the wrong work.
- The right initial team is three functions: product management, interaction design, and prototyping (the founder may cover PM, and a designer may cover prototyper, but all three functions must be present).
- Discovery is iterative: expect literally dozens of versions of the prototype, with each iteration getting closer to a winning product.
- A validated high-fidelity prototype acts as a "living spec" that lets engineering produce a quality implementation far faster than they otherwise could.
- The same model applies in larger companies — they just have the cash to absorb the inefficiency of doing it wrong; startups don't.

## Practices
- Hire (or assign founders to) PM, interaction designer, and prototyper *before* hiring an engineering team.
- Run rapid product discovery with two non-negotiables: (1) build a high-fidelity prototype that mimics the eventual user experience, and (2) validate the product design with real target users.
- Treat the discovery phase as taking "a few weeks to a few months" — the explicit goal is to arrive at a validated product before burning $500K or more in seed funding.
- Iterate the prototype daily — sometimes with minor refinements, sometimes with major changes — until the target market signals it's a winner.
- Once discovery is done, hand engineering three deliverables: a product validated with the target market, a rich prototype that serves as a living spec, and a much greater understanding of what it will take to succeed.

## Pitfalls
- The "engineering-driven industry" default: jumping straight to hiring engineers because that's just how things start.
- Operating in "stealth mode" so long that there is little customer interaction during the period when customer interaction matters most.
- The founder/CEO acting as both product manager and product designer without the discovery process to back it up — definite ideas are not the same as a validated product.
- Trying to save money by outsourcing engineering off-shore — same process, same problems, same outcome.
- Burning through the seed round to build the wrong thing, then trying to raise more capital to fix a product that hasn't yet been discovered.

## Notable Frameworks / Definitions
- **The two keys of rapid product discovery.** (1) Create a high-fidelity prototype that mimics the eventual user experience. (2) Validate this product design with real target users.
- **The three deliverables at the end of the discovery phase.** (a) A product that you have validated with the target market; (b) a very rich prototype that serves as a living spec for the engineering team to build from; (c) a much greater understanding of what you're getting into, and what you'll need to do to succeed.
- **The three functions on the pre-engineering team.** Product management, interaction design, and prototyping — sometimes the designer covers prototyping, sometimes the founder covers product management, but the three functions must all be present before any code is written.
