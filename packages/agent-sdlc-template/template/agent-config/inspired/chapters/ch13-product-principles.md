# Chapter 13: Product Principles

## Premise
Beyond strategy and roadmap, a product team needs an explicit, prioritized
declaration of what it believes — its product principles. Principles are
durable, not tied to any single release, and they exist to make the thousand
in-the-weeds tradeoffs faster and more consistent across the team.

## Key Principles
- Product principles are a public declaration of the team's beliefs and
  intentions. Done well, they complement strategy and accelerate discovery.
- Principles are not features and are not tied to any product incarnation —
  they sit at the level of a product strategy for the entire product line, or a
  startup's mission statement.
- Coming up with principles is the act of deciding what is strategic and
  fundamental vs. tactical and temporary — what's important vs. incidental.
- Principles must be prioritized, not just listed. Many products want to be both
  easy to use and safe and secure; what matters is which one wins when they
  conflict.
- More than any other document, a good principles document brings product
  management, UX design, engineering, and marketing onto the same page.
- Escalating contested product decisions to a senior manager is a bad result.
  You want the debate and arguments, with everyone on board at the end; the
  senior-manager override breeds resentment and produces a worse product.

## Practices
- Get the team in a room for a couple of hours to discuss, identify, and
  prioritize what they believe matters.
- Decide whether to publish the principles. Sometimes they're an internal tool
  like a strategy doc; sometimes they're a public statement to users,
  customers, partners, suppliers, investors, and employees.
- For every contested product decision, first frame it explicitly: What problem
  exactly are we solving? For which persona? What are the goals? What is the
  relative priority of each goal?
- Force a specific ordering of goals (most to least important). Don't bucket
  them into "critical" and "very important" — be able to say what's first, what's
  second, what's third.
- Put the goals and priorities on the whiteboard before evaluating options so
  everyone has the same evaluation framework in view.
- Make the decision process completely transparent — every team member should
  see the goals, priorities, and how each option was assessed.

## Pitfalls
- Writing principles so generic they aren't useful ("must be reliable").
- Confusing product principles with design principles. "Provide a well-lit path
  so the user always knows where to go next" is a design principle, not a
  product principle.
- Skipping principle-setting and prioritization, then watching every product
  decision become a passionate argument with no common basis of evaluation.
- Letting the team think the PM is just "following intuition." If the reasoning
  isn't visible, the decision won't stick.
- Misreading disagreement as factual disagreement when it's almost always about
  differing interpretations or weightings of the goals.

## Notable Frameworks / Definitions
- **Product principle.** A prioritized, durable belief about what matters in
  this product line — independent of any specific release or feature. Example
  given: for a movie site, the team believes the user community's opinions on
  movies are more valuable than those of professional reviewers. Later, when a
  studio asks to place its reviews on the site, the principle resolves the call.
- **Decision frame (for resolving conflicts).** Before debating options, agree
  on: (1) the exact problem, (2) the persona it's for, (3) the goals, and (4)
  the priority of each goal.
- **Drive-bys.** A manager who drops in occasionally, shoots down progress,
  and disappears without the feedback that would help address the concerns —
  a failure mode product principles and explicit decision frames are meant to
  blunt.
