# Chapter 23: Improving Existing Products

## Premise
Most product organizations are essentially "feature factories (with some bug fixing thrown in)" — they treat product improvement as "what do we add next?" That orientation usually makes products worse, not better. Real improvement of an existing product comes from naming the business metrics that matter and relentlessly working those numbers using live data, real-user testing, and disciplined hypothesis-driven changes.

## Key Principles
- Improving an existing product is not about adding features. New features are justified only by the degree to which they can move the metrics you care about.
- It starts with a very clear understanding of what you're trying to achieve. The business outcome — e.g., raising a registration-completion rate from 7% to 15% — drives everything else.
- The PM's job is to live and breathe the metrics. Every day, ask what you could do to move them.
- The biggest gains often aren't in new features at all. Common levers include UX changes, asking for less personal information, explaining *why* the information is needed, and earning trust with the data being collected.
- Don't fall into the trap of gathering subjective feedback, eliciting customer "requirements," chasing features, or running surveys and focus groups. What matters is what actually moves the needle.
- Internet services in particular have near-real-time data — improvements can be tested and seen quickly and dramatically.
- This kind of work is among the most rewarding a PM can do — fast feedback, visible business impact, and immediate validation that you moved the needle.

## Practices
- Instrument the funnel and study it. For an online insurance application: how many users visit the start of the application, how many drop off at each page, how many refuse to give personal information, how many confirm their email, how many complete the process.
- Work closely with an interaction designer, user researcher, and lead engineer to consider possibilities — not in isolation as a PM with a feature wish-list.
- Use multiple input sources to understand what's going on: site analytics, real-user testing on the product, NPS tracking, customer service, the sales force, and win/loss analysis.
- Justify each candidate change by the metric it is expected to move. Ship, measure, and decide whether it actually moved the number.
- For an example metric move (the chapter's running case): driving an application-completion rate from 7% to 15% is the kind of single-number outcome the whole roadmap can be organized around.

## Pitfalls
- The "feature factory" orientation — measuring success in features shipped rather than outcomes improved.
- Roadmaps loaded with "if you add these six features, I'll buy" demands from specific customers, treated as the improvement plan.
- Letting surveys, focus groups, or single customer voices set the agenda. They're not the same as what actually moves the metrics.
- Adding features that "make the situation worse and not better" — common when adds are not tied to a measurable outcome.

## Notable Frameworks / Definitions
- **Feature factory.** Cagan's label for product orgs whose default mode is "add the next feature (with some bug fixing thrown in)." It is the anti-pattern this chapter pushes against.
- **The metric-driven loop.** Pick the right business metrics for the product (page views, registered users, conversion, subscriptions, revenue, etc.). Decide what counts as success and what counts as a problem. Live in the data daily. Justify each candidate change by the metric it should move. Watch what moves.
