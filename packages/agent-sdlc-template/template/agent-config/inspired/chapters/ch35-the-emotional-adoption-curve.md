# Chapter 35: The Emotional Adoption Curve

## Premise
Geoffrey Moore's technology adoption curve labels groups (innovators, early
adopters, etc.) but doesn't tell you what to design for. In an interview, Jeff
Bonforte re-labels each group by its driving emotion — Lover, Irrational,
Efficient, Laugher, Comfortable — and argues that products win by tapping into
the latent anger and frustration of the Irrationals, not by chasing the
technically curious Lovers.

## Key Principles
- "Angry people dictate the future of technology." Focus product managers on the
  most miserable everyday problem you can change — that is where the truly big
  wins come from.
- Lovers (Innovators) adopt because they find the technology cool. They are the
  most dangerous group to listen to because their motivations don't generalize
  to anyone else.
- Irrationals (Early Adopters) feel the same emotion as the mainstream but with
  far more intensity. They will spend irrationally — more time or money than the
  rational benefit warrants — because they're passionate about the problem.
- Confusing Irrationals with Lovers is one of the main reasons startups fall
  into the chasm. The Lovers love your tech; the Irrationals love your solution
  to their problem. Only the latter teaches you the value of the product all the
  way down the line.
- Irrationals slingshot a product across the chasm into the Efficients and
  Laughers. Without the underlying emotional driver, there is no slingshot —
  which is why adding video to Skype barely moved webcam usage: no one is angry
  at their webcam.
- Emotional groups are not demographic groups. The Irrationals for a finance
  product might be retired women, not the assumed 18-30 males. Look at each
  product's actual emotional drivers.
- The lower the need on Maslow's pyramid, the deeper the emotion, and the
  easier it is to motivate behavior change. Politicians tap fear for this same
  reason; aspirational appeals are much harder to act on.

## Practices
- Hunt deliberately for spaces with high latent anger, exasperation, and
  frustration — telcos, banks, consumer credit, the tax authority, government
  bureaucracy, airlines, healthcare. These are innovation goldmines.
- For each project, ask: which core emotions does this appeal to, and how acute
  is the user's pain?
- Use the "freshman test": tap into the emotions of the first day of high
  school — loneliness, insecurity, fear, frustration, anger. If your product
  speaks to one of those universal feelings, you are on the right track.
- When a large company envies startup-style "cool stuff," remember which
  emotional group is actually your base; don't kowtow to Lovers when your
  customers are Laughers.

## Pitfalls
- Designing for the loudest, most technical group (the Lovers) and concluding
  you have product-market fit.
- Treating Moore's curve as design guidance — the labels describe behavior, not
  what to build.
- Assuming demographic similarity inside an emotional group; the Irrationals
  for a product are defined by the emotion, not the demographic.
- Picking categories with no latent frustration (e.g., webcams) and being
  surprised when growth stalls.

## Notable Frameworks / Definitions
- **Lovers (Innovators):** techies who buy because the technology is cool;
  motivations don't generalize.
- **Irrationals (Early Adopters):** feel mainstream emotions at multiplier
  intensity; spend irrationally to address the emotional need.
- **Efficients (Early Majority):** same emotion, pragmatic on benefits vs. costs;
  adopt when the technology becomes practical.
- **Laughers (Late Majority):** muted version of the same emotion; will not
  tolerate any grief to get the benefits.
- **Comfortable (Laggards):** want the benefits only when the experience is
  drop-dead simple and convenient.
