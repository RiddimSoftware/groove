# Chapter 10: Managing Up

## Premise
The biggest frustration PMs report — especially in large companies — is shifting
direction from management. Conflicting weekly guidance, competing stakeholder
agendas, and external pressures (competitors, M&A, business development,
budget) all guarantee some level of churn. The challenges won't disappear, but
a disciplined set of techniques can substantially mitigate them.

## Key Principles
- Churn is a feature of large-company product work, not a defect to be
  eliminated. Plan for it instead of being surprised by it.
- Managers are not interchangeable. Adapt to your manager's preferred
  communication style, even when it isn't yours.
- The formal meeting is the ratification step, not the alignment step.
  Alignment happens one-on-one before the meeting.
- Bring recommendations, not problems. Ideally bring options, a recommendation,
  and the rationale.
- Use data and facts, not opinions — because, as Jim Barksdale said,
  "If we're going to make this decision based on opinions, we're going to use
  my opinion."
- Be a low-maintenance employee. Managers won't say it out loud, but it's what
  they want most.
- Evangelism is part of the job, not a side activity — and most PMs don't take
  it seriously enough.

## Practices
Cagan's ten techniques for managing up:
1. **Measure and plan for churn.** Track how much of each week/month/quarter
   goes to forward progress vs. rework; schedule with the expectation that some
   portion of effort will end up on the shelf.
2. **Communication style and frequency.** Learn whether your manager wants
   continuous updates or escalation-only, written detail or a hallway chat —
   and meet that preference.
3. **Pre-meeting work.** Run the real meeting one-on-one with key influencers
   and stakeholders before the group meeting. The formal meeting should be
   quick and surprise-free; its purpose is for everyone to see that everyone
   else is on board.
4. **Recommendations, not issues.** Bring an analysis of alternatives plus a
   recommendation and rationale, scaled to the size of the decision.
5. **Use your manager.** Ask your manager to get the access you can't —
   for example, by holding a private pre-brief with a senior stakeholder who
   won't make time for you.
6. **Do your homework.** Managers spot holes in thinking quickly; preparation
   is the defense.
7. **Short e-mails.** The more senior the recipient, the shorter the message.
   Offer supporting material; never require it to be read.
8. **Use data and facts, not opinions.** Lay out the data so the
   recommendation is obvious without invoking authority.
9. **Evangelize.** Make the product known and exciting across the company;
   other groups will then find ways to help.
10. **Low-maintenance employees.** Don't use your direct manager as your
    mentor — find one outside the management chain — and be deliberate about
    how you spend their time.

## Pitfalls
- Treating churn as a personal failure rather than as a structural cost of
  large-company work.
- Walking into a status meeting expecting to drive alignment in the room. By
  then it's too late.
- Long, detailed e-mails to senior managers who get hundreds a day, then
  resenting that they go unread.
- Using your direct manager as a mentor. The role conflict makes you
  high-maintenance and burns the relationship you actually need to preserve.
- Bringing problems without analysis or options, and expecting your manager to
  diagnose.
