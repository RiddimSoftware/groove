# Chapter 14: The Product Council

## Premise
Every product company needs a single mechanism for making timely, informed
product decisions across functions. The Product Council — a small,
cross-functional standing body — provides that oversight at four explicit
milestones, replacing endless ad hoc meetings without sliding into executive
micromanagement.

## Key Principles
- The Product Council sets strategic product direction, allocates product
  resources, and oversees product efforts. It does not set business strategy
  and does not design products.
- The council exists to give management the visibility and oversight it owes
  the business — without the disempowerment that comes from execs trying to
  design products themselves.
- Effectiveness is largely a function of the leader's meeting skills: staying
  on task, framing issues, and driving to decisions. The leader is usually the
  head of product, or for smaller companies the head of the company.
- The product manager — not their manager — should present their product to
  the council, after individually pre-briefing council members to surface and
  resolve issues before the formal meeting.
- Cost estimates need different fidelity at different milestones. Anything
  beyond "small, medium, or large" before the solution exists isn't fair to
  engineering; by Milestone 3, the estimate must be detailed enough for the
  team to commit to.

## Practices
- Keep membership cross-functional and at 10 or fewer. A typical roster:
  CEO/COO/GM, VP/Director of Product Management, UX Design, Marketing,
  Engineering, Site Operations, Customer Service.
- When more people want in, assign them a representative on the council (e.g.
  VP Sales is represented by VP Marketing; head of QA by VP Engineering).
- Skip minor updates and fixes — run them through a separate expedited process
  for the smaller, often content-related changes the business needs day-to-day.
- Keep policy discussions (end-of-life, privacy, etc.) from crowding out product
  oversight; decide deliberately whether the council owns them.
- 3 to 6 months post-launch, review the actual business performance of shipped
  products in the council, so members learn which of their bets paid off.
- For large companies, run one council per business unit. For small companies,
  one council typically covers all products. Meeting cadence ranges from one
  hour a month to two hours a week depending on workload.

## Pitfalls
- Letting the council become a product design session. If a product has issues,
  the team works on them and returns when they're resolved.
- Taking Milestone 2 cost estimates literally — they're SWAGs by definition.
  Acting on them invites later sticker shock.
- Allowing unstructured policy discussions to derail product oversight.
- Skipping the council and letting decisions languish across countless other
  meetings — the cost the council is designed to eliminate.

## Notable Frameworks / Definitions
The four Product Council milestones for every product effort:

1. **Milestone 1** — Review proposed product strategies and roadmaps; initiate
   opportunity assessments for specific releases (select the opportunities to
   investigate).
2. **Milestone 2** — Review opportunity assessments and recommendations;
   issue go/no-go to begin discovering a solution.
3. **Milestone 3** — Review prototypes, user testing results, and detailed cost
   estimates; issue go/no-go to begin engineering.
4. **Milestone 4** — Review final product, QA status, launch plans, and
   community impact assessments; issue go/no-go to launch.

**Estimation cadence.** A preliminary "small / medium / large" estimate at
Milestone 2 (opportunity assessment), followed by a detailed, high-confidence
estimate at Milestone 3 alongside the final product spec.

The product-council concept as described originates with Maynard Webb, former
COO of eBay.
