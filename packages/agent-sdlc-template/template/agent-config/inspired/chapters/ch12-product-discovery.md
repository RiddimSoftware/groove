# Chapter 12: Product Discovery

## Premise
Software projects have two distinct stages: figuring out what to build (discovery)
and building it (execution). Most teams collapse them — they schedule discovery
like construction and let engineering act as a very expensive prototype shop on
live customers. Discovery is fundamentally a creative process and must be run as
one, with a hard mindset shift to execution once the spec is set.

## Key Principles
- The product invention process is fundamentally a creative process — more art
  than science. It cannot be scheduled like the construction of a house.
- Discovery has two jobs: validate the market (real users want this) and discover
  a solution that is valuable, usable, and feasible.
- Once a release enters engineering, the product manager must shift to execution
  mode. Continuing to chase new ideas mid-build is the primary source of "churn"
  — slipped dates, cut features, compromised quality.
- Talking to users, prototyping, and testing are worthless if you don't adjust
  course based on what you learn.
- Using the full engineering team to "just build something" is in fact building a
  very, very expensive prototype, with live customers as unwitting test subjects.
  This is why companies routinely take three releases over one to two years before
  a product makes money.
- Startups especially cannot afford this — most don't have two years of runway to
  burn before finding traction.

## Practices
- Run two releases in parallel: as engineering enters execution on release 1.0,
  start discovery on release 2.0. Redirect creative urges to the next release so
  new exec asks don't disturb the one in the oven.
- During discovery, use one prototyper for a few weeks instead of the full
  engineering team for full release cycles.
- Keep engineers involved during discovery (evaluating options, preparing
  infrastructure) rather than hiring more headcount upfront.
- Help management understand discovery by consistently re-emphasizing the
  obligation: what engineering builds must be valuable and usable, and discovery
  is how we ensure that.
- Develop both skill sets deliberately. Discovery-natured PMs must build execution
  discipline; execution-natured PMs must build strategic and discovery skills.

## Pitfalls
- Treating product spec as malleable deep into engineering — "churn" by another
  name.
- Pushing forward with a spec the prototype already showed users don't want or
  can't figure out, because "time is up and the engineers are ready." The result
  ships, and management blames the PM.
- Letting fear of idle, expensive engineers force premature commitments — the
  very thinking that wastes the most engineering capacity.
- Scheduling discovery as a fixed sequential phase like "requirements and design"
  before the build.

## Notable Frameworks / Definitions
- **Discovery vs. Execution.** Two distinct stages of a project. Discovery =
  "build the right product"; execution = "build the product right." Each requires
  a different mindset, and the shift between them must be deliberate.
- **Valuable, Usable, Feasible.** The three properties a discovered solution must
  have. Valuable and usable come from customer validation; feasible comes from
  the engineering team.
- **Two parallel releases.** Always keep two versions of a product going in
  parallel — release N in execution while release N+1 is in discovery — so the
  innovation engine never stops and new requirements have a natural home.
