# Chapter 13: Guide for the Perplexed

*Making usability happen where you live.*

(The original *Guide for the Perplexed* is Maimonides' 12th-century commentary on the Talmud — Krug just thinks it's the best title he's ever heard.)

The chapter answers a recurring email: *"OK, I get it. Usability is important and I want to do it. How do I convince my boss to take users seriously and let me spend time on it?"*

## Ya gotta know the territory

Late 1990s: the terms were **Usability** and **Information Architecture**. Two professions. Two skill sets.

Now: **User Experience Design (UX, UXD)** is the umbrella. Under it sit Interaction Design, Interface Design, Visual Design, Content Management, Usability, Information Architecture, and a dozen other specialties.

UX broadens scope from "design the product well" to "consider users at every stage of the product lifecycle."

- **Good news:** UX is an easier sell now. Steve Jobs and Jonathan Ive made a compelling business case.
- **Bad news:** Usability used to be the standard bearer for user-friendly design; now it's one sibling at a crowded table.
- **Worse news:** Most people don't understand the differences between the specialties or what each contributes.

When someone says "I'm in UX" or "Usability is so 2002 — it's all UX now," smile and ask how they're learning about users, how they're testing whether people can use what they're building, and how they get changes to happen. If they don't do any of those, they need help. If they do, learn from them.

## The usual advice for getting buy-in

The two standard moves:

- **Demonstrate ROI.** Gather data showing your usability work created savings or revenue. (See Bias & Mayhew, *Cost-justifying Usability*.) Worthwhile if you can pull it off, but a lot of work, and there'll always be someone arguing the gain came from something else.
- **Speak their language.** Talk in management vocabulary — pain points, touch points, KPIs, CSI — to make clear you're part of the solution.

Both fine. But ROI is hard work to nail down, and learning to speak business is its own undertaking (that's what MBAs are for).

## If I were you…

Krug's own recommendations, having spent time visiting client offices:

### Get your boss (and her boss) to watch a usability test

The single most effective tactic. Tell them you're doing some testing and it would help team morale if they could poke their head in for a few minutes. Executives often **stay longer than planned** — it's the first time they've watched anyone try to use the company's site, and it's almost always less pretty than they imagined.

Live attendance matters. Watching a test live ≠ hearing a recap. (Like watching a sporting event live vs. evening news.) If you can't get them in person, settle for highlight clips in a presentation, or post a short (under 3 min) clip on the intranet with an intriguing description.

### Do the first one on your own time

Don't ask for permission for your first test. Keep it incredibly simple. Find volunteers. Pick an easy target — something you *know* is a problem and can fix quickly without political ceremony (a poorly-labelled button). Test it, fix it, publicise the result. If you can measure the improvement (reduced support calls, for example), do.

### Do a test of the competition

Easier to get buy-in for a test where your site isn't on the line. People love watching the competition. Great brown-bag lunch event.

### Empathise with management

Empathy is virtually a professional requirement for usability work. Apply it to your bosses — not in the "how do I manipulate them into doing what I want" way, but in the "understand the position they find themselves in" way. Real emotional empathy. You may be surprised by the effect.

### Know your place in the grand scheme of things

A little humility goes a long way. In any business almost everyone is a small cog. You want your enthusiasm to be infectious — but going around as the bringer of truth about usability doesn't work. Your primary job is to *share* what you know, not to *tell* people how things should be done.

### Two books worth reading

- **Tomer Sharon, *It's Our Research: Getting Stakeholder Buy-In for User Experience Research Projects*.** Chapters with titles like "Become the voice of reason" and "Accept the fact that it might not work and that it's okay" tell you the vibe.
- **Leah Buley, *The User Experience Team of One: A Research and Design Survival Guide*.** Written for solo UX practitioners. Chapter 3 (building support) and Chapter 4 (growing yourself and career) are the standouts.

## Resist the dark forces

Usability is fundamentally a *user-advocate* job. Like the Lorax, you speak for the trees — well, the users. Build better products by serving people better.

There's a trend Krug noticed about five years before this edition: usability practitioners getting asked to help *manipulate* users instead of serve them.

- **Benign manipulation:** a slightly hidden newsletter checkbox, pre-checked.
- **Closer to dark:** tricking installs of unwanted browser toolbars or default-search changes.
- **True black hat:** phishing, scamming, identity theft.

(There's even a book about it — *Evil by Design* — organised around the Seven Deadly Sins.)

Helping people *influence* users is fine. Cialdini's *Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion* and Weinschenk's books on persuasion-from-neuropsychology are great reads. Think-aloud usability tests are useful for understanding why attempts at persuasion succeed or fail.

But Krug gets anxious when usability is invoked to test whether something is *desirable* — that's a market-research question, best answered with market-research tools. Worse, the underlying request is often "make people *think* it's desirable" — i.e. manipulate them.

> If people ask you to do any of this, it's not part of your job. **The users are counting on you.**

## A few definitive answers

Almost everything in the book has been "it depends." Here's a tiny collection of things that should always (or never) be done:

- **Don't use small, low-contrast type.** Large low-contrast OK; small high-contrast OK. Never small *and* low-contrast.
- **Don't put labels inside form fields.** Tempting on cramped mobile screens. Only do it if the form is exceptionally simple, labels disappear and re-appear correctly when the field is emptied, labels can't be confused with answers, the field text can never end up submitted ("Job Tit*Assistant Manager*tle"), and they're fully accessible.
- **Preserve the distinction between visited and unvisited links.** Browsers track by URL, so two differently-worded links to the same page should still show as visited. You can use any colours, as long as they're noticeably different.
- **Don't float headings between paragraphs.** Headings belong closer to the section *below* than the one *above*. (Mentioned in Chapter 3 but worth repeating — important enough.)

## Closing

Building a great Web site or app is an enormous challenge. Anyone who gets it even half right has Krug's admiration. Don't take anything in the book as forbidding rule-breaking — there are sites where you *want* the interface to make people think. Just know which rules you're bending and have a good reason.

Above all, be of good cheer.
