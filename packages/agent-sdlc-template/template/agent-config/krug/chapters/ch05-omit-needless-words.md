# Chapter 5: Omit Needless Words

*The art of not writing for the Web.*

## Krug's Third Law of Usability

> "Get rid of half the words on each page, then get rid of half of what's left."

Lifted in spirit from E. B. White's rule 17 in *The Elements of Style*:

> Vigorous writing is concise. A sentence should contain no unnecessary words, a paragraph no unnecessary sentences, for the same reason that a drawing should have no unnecessary lines and a machine no unnecessary parts.

The "half, then half again" target is deliberately extreme. Getting rid of half is achievable on most pages without losing anything of value; the second half is the prompt to keep being ruthless. Most pages will still have more words than they need after one pass.

## Why words matter so much on the Web

Even words no one will read still:

- Take up space.
- Add to the noise level of the page.
- Hide the useful content.
- Make pages appear more daunting than they are.

Cutting them yields three concrete wins:

1. Lower noise level.
2. The useful content stands out more.
3. Pages get shorter — users see more at a glance, less scrolling.

This isn't an argument that *all* Web text should be brief; long-form articles on WebMD or *NYTimes.com* belong as long as they need to be. But certain genres of Web writing are particularly bloated.

## Happy talk must die

> Happy talk is content-free, basically just a way to be sociable. But most Web users don't have time for small talk.

Definition: the introductory text that welcomes you to the site or section and tells you how great it is or what you're about to see. The sure-fire test: if you can hear a faint "blah blah blah blah blah…" in your head while reading it, it's happy talk.

Common habitats:

- **Home pages**, especially paragraphs starting with "Welcome to…".
- **Section fronts** (the top page of a section). These often have no real content of their own and get filled with happy talk for the sake of having "something."

It's the equivalent of a publisher adding a paragraph to a book's table-of-contents page saying *"This book contains many interesting chapters about ___, ___, and ___. We hope you enjoy them."*

You can — and should — eliminate as much as possible.

## Instructions must die

The main fact about instructions: **no one is going to read them**, at least not until repeated attempts to muddle through have failed. Even then, if the instructions are wordy, the odds of finding the needed info are low.

The objective: eliminate instructions entirely by making the design self-explanatory. When that's truly not possible, cut to the bare minimum.

### Before-and-after example

A site-survey intro Krug saw, 103 words long, told users:

- The questionnaire is to improve the site (happy talk).
- How to use drop-downs and radio buttons (users already know).
- That it takes 2-3 minutes (useful — but easy to miss in the bloat).
- That at the bottom they can leave their contact info (belongs at the bottom).
- That for comments needing a response they should contact Customer Service (useful — but no link).

Krug's 34-word rewrite:

> Please help us improve the site by taking 2-3 minutes to complete this survey.
>
> NOTE: If you have comments or concerns that require a response, don't use this form. Instead, please contact [Customer Service].

Same information transmitted with one-third the words and a usable link at the relevant moment.
