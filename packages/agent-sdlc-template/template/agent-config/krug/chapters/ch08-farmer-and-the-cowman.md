# Chapter 8: "The Farmer and the Cowman Should Be Friends"

*Why most arguments about usability are a waste of time, and how to avoid them.*

> "One man likes to push a plough / The other likes to chase a cow / But that's no reason why they can't be friends!" — *Oklahoma!*

Left to themselves, Web teams aren't good at making usability decisions. They rehash the same questions endlessly — "religious debates" that look a lot like religion or politics: strongly held personal beliefs about things that can't be proven, rarely changing anyone's view.

## "Everybody likes ___."

All of us who work on Web sites are Web users. As users, we have strong personal preferences. As team members, we have a hard time setting them aside, and a natural tendency to project: *most users are like me*.

The result is a room full of people with strong convictions, each thinking they know what's right. We don't actually believe *everyone* is like us — but we believe most *sensible* people are.

## Farmers vs. cowmen

On top of personal preference is professional bias. Like the farmers and cowmen of *Oklahoma!*, different roles have different definitions of "good design":

- **CEOs** want the site to "have pizzazz."
- **Developers** like complexity — they enjoy reverse-engineering how things work and showing off clever solutions.
- **Designers** love elegant visual experiences — there are actual endorphins involved.
- **Business development** wants the site to drive deals.

Designers want sites that look great. Developers want sites with ingenious features. These reactions are visceral, brain-chemical-level — hard to imagine anyone feeling differently.

A larger clash sits behind this: the **hype culture** (management, marketing, BD) makes promises to win deals; the **craft culture** (designers, developers) has to deliver. Art Kleiner described this as a recurring tension. Edicts come down ("Make everything twice as big — in time for the trade show next week").

## The myth of the Average User

When the team hits stalemate, the conversation usually turns to figuring out what *most* users want. Expert opinions, surveys, focus groups — anything to find out what the Average Web User is really like.

But:

> **There is no Average User.**
>
> **All Web users are unique, and all Web use is basically idiosyncratic.**

Individual reactions depend on so many variables that one-dimensional "users like / don't like X" claims are futile and counter-productive.

The worst thing about the myth of the Average User is that it makes Web design feel like a matter of figuring out what people like. It's not. There are no simple "right" answers to most design questions. **What works is good, integrated design — carefully thought out, well executed, and tested.**

(That's not to say nothing is ever wrong. There are wrong ways to design pages — they're just not usually the things teams argue about.)

## The antidote: testing

The right question is never "Do most people like pull-down menus?" The right question is

> "Does *this* pull-down, with *these* items and *this* wording, in *this* context on *this* page, create a good experience for most people who are likely to use *this* site?"

There's only one way to answer that: **build some version of the thing (even crude), watch people try to use it.**

Where religious debates burn time and erode respect, usability testing tends to defuse them — it moves the conversation away from "what's right" and toward "what works." And by showing how varied users' reactions actually are, testing makes it hard to keep believing all users are like us.
