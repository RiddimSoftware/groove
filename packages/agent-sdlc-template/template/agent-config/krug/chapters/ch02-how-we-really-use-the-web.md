# Chapter 2: How We *Really* Use the Web

*Scanning, satisficing, and muddling through.*

## What designers imagine vs. what actually happens

We design as if users will pore over each page, read our carefully crafted text, weigh options, and decide.

What users actually do most of the time:

- Glance at the page.
- Scan some of the text.
- Click the first link that catches their interest or vaguely resembles what they want.
- Ignore large parts of the page entirely.

The mental model designers should use is not "great literature" or even "product brochure" — it's **"billboard going by at 60 miles an hour."**

## Three facts of life

### Fact #1: We don't read pages. We scan them.

We tend to spend very little time *reading* most Web pages. We scan for words or phrases that catch the eye.

Why?

- **We're usually on a mission.** Web use tends to be goal-directed. Users act like sharks — keep moving or die.
- **We know we don't need to read everything.** On most pages, only a fraction is relevant to the current task.
- **We're good at it.** Scanning is a basic skill — we've been scanning newspapers, magazines, books, Twitter, and Reddit our whole lives.

What we *see* on a page depends on what we *have in mind*. Like Gary Larson's Ginger cartoon, we hear only the words that match our task ("Book a trip…") and our standing interests, plus hardwired triggers like *Free*, *Sale*, *Sex*, and our own name.

### Fact #2: We don't make optimal choices. We satisfice.

We assume users will scan all options and pick the best. In reality, users pick the **first reasonable option** — a strategy named *satisficing* by Herbert Simon (a portmanteau of *satisfying* + *sufficing*).

Gary Klein's research on naturalistic decision making (*Sources of Power*) confirmed it: even fire commanders in high-stakes situations don't compare options. They take the first plausible plan and mentally pressure-test it.

Why satisficing works on the Web:

- **We're in a hurry.** Optimising is hard and slow.
- **The penalty for guessing wrong is small.** One Back-button click. (Back is the most-used button in the browser.)
- **Weighing options often doesn't help.** On a poorly designed site, more thought doesn't yield a better choice.
- **Guessing is more fun.** Less work, possibility of pleasant surprise.

### Fact #3: We don't figure out how things work. We muddle through.

Most users — including technically savvy ones — use software, sites, and household appliances with **vaguely wrong models of how they work** and never read the instructions.

Why?

- **It's not important to us.** We don't care how it works as long as it works.
- **If we find something that works, we stick to it.** A better way exists, but we rarely look for it.

This is hard for developers to internalise — *they* care how things work — but it's reality. Like the Pauper in *The Prince and the Pauper*, who used the Great Seal of England as a nutcracker because to him it was just a heavy chunk of metal, users will use your site in ways you never intended.

Muddling through *works*, but it's inefficient and error-prone. When users actually *get it*, they:

- Find what they came for more often.
- Understand the full range of what you offer.
- Are easier to steer to the parts you want them to see.
- Feel smarter, more in control, and come back.

## Design implications

If your audience is going to act like you're designing billboards, **design great billboards.** (See Chapter 3.)

Don't try to convert users into careful readers, attentive option-weighers, or curious learners. They will not become any of those things. Design for the user who's scanning, satisficing, and muddling — and the small minority who do read carefully will find your pages even easier to use.
