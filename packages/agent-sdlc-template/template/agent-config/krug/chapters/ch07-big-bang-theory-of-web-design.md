# Chapter 7: The Big Bang Theory of Web Design

*The importance of getting people off on the right foot.*

The Home page (and any landing page) has a lot to do, under adverse conditions. Treat the first few seconds as critical — what users decide there shapes how they read everything that follows.

## What the Home page has to do

Concrete jobs:

- **Site identity and mission.** What is this site, and why am I here, not somewhere else?
- **Site hierarchy.** Overview of content ("what's here?") and features ("what can I do?"), usually via persistent navigation.
- **Search.** Almost always a prominent search box.
- **Teases.** Like a magazine cover, hint at the good stuff inside.
- **Content promos** — newest, best, most popular items (top stories, hot deals).
- **Feature promos** — invitations to explore additional sections or features.
- **Timely content** — for return-visit sites; even a press-release link signals life on quieter sites.
- **Deals** — ads, cross-promotions, co-branding.
- **Shortcuts** — frequently-requested items get their own links (software updates, etc.).
- **Registration / sign-in** — clear paths for new and returning users.

Abstract objectives:

- **Show me what I'm looking for** (if it's on the site).
- **…and what I'm *not* looking for** — surface things I'd be interested in but didn't think to look for.
- **Show me where to start.**
- **Establish credibility and trust** — for many visitors, this is the only chance.

…and you have to do it all under adverse conditions:

- **Everyone wants a piece of it.** Home-page real estate is "waterfront property" — turf wars are fierce. The "above the fold" space is even more contested.
- **Too many cooks.** Even the CEO has an opinion about the Home page.
- **One size fits all.** Has to appeal to every visitor segment.

## The first casualty of war: conveying the big picture

When compromises pile up, the thing most often lost is **what the site actually is**. Designers are too close to the site to remember that it isn't obvious to everyone.

The Home page needs to answer, at a glance, the four questions a new visitor brings:

- **What is this?**
- **What can I do here?**
- **What do they have here?**
- **Why should I be here — and not somewhere else?**

If users misinterpret in the first few seconds, they'll force-fit that wrong interpretation onto everything they see next, compounding the confusion.

This is the **Big Bang Theory of Web Design**: the first few seconds are critical. A 2006 experiment ("Attention Web Designers: You Have 50 Milliseconds to Make a Good First Impression!") found that initial impressions in milliseconds are very similar to deeper assessments. Snap judgments, but durable.

## Top four plausible excuses for not spelling out the big picture

| Excuse | Reality |
| :--- | :--- |
| "It's obvious." | Obvious to you, not to everyone. |
| "After people see it once, they'll find it annoying." | Almost no one is offended by a brief reminder of what a site is. |
| "Anybody who really needs it will know what it is." | Many people who need the site *don't* know what it is at first. |
| "That's what our advertising is for." | Even if ads worked perfectly, users forget which ad brought them by the time they arrive. |

## But — the Home page? Really?

Yes, even though most users now enter the site via deep-link from email or social rather than the Home page. Two reasons:

- Every page should orient them — so most pages need to do *some* of the Home page's work.
- The Home page is still where users "bob to the surface" after a deep-link visit, to figure out what site they're on and what else is here.

## How to get the big picture across

Three explicit slots designed to answer "what is this?":

- **The tagline.** Phrase right next to/below the Site ID. *Treated as a description of the whole site.*
- **The Welcome blurb.** A terse description, usually top-left or centre of the content area — first thing users encounter.
- **The "Learn more."** Innovative offerings need more explaining. People are now used to short explanatory videos.

Most users won't use all three, but you want at least one to anchor them, and a clearly labelled place to go if they want to find out.

Guidelines:

- **Use as much space as necessary** — Kickstarter sensibly devotes most of its Home page to explaining what it is.
- **…but not more.** Don't try to list every great feature; pick a few important ones.
- **Don't use a mission statement as a Welcome blurb.** Nobody reads "XYZCorp offers world-class solutions in the burgeoning field of blah…".
- **Test it.** You can't trust your own judgment about whether the big picture lands. Show the Home page to outsiders.

## Nothing beats a good tagline

A tagline is a pithy phrase characterising the whole enterprise, sitting next to the Site ID. It's one of the most efficient ways to convey a site's value proposition.

Traits of a good tagline:

- **Clear and informative.** Says what the site does.
- **Just long enough, but not too long.** Six to eight words is usually right.
- **Conveys differentiation and benefit.** Jakob Nielsen: a really good tagline is one *no one else in the world could use except you*.
- **Personable, lively, sometimes clever** — only if clever helps clarity, not obscures it.

Bad taglines are **generic** ("Save Time. Save Money. Save Your Sanity.") or are **mottos** ("We bring good things to life," "To protect and to serve"). A motto expresses a guiding principle; a tagline conveys a value proposition. If I don't know what the thing is, a motto won't tell me.

Sites with household-name brands (NYTimes, Coke) can arguably skip a tagline. But even they benefit, because the online mission is rarely identical to the offline brand, and the chance to explain the difference is cheap.

## The fifth question: Where do I start?

After the first four "what is this" questions, users need to know how to *begin*. From a glance at the Home page, they should be able to say:

- **Here's where to start if I want to search.**
- **Here's where to start if I want to browse.**
- **Here's where to start if I want to sample the best stuff.**

Step-by-step sites (mortgage applications) need the entry point of the process to leap out. Sites with new-user/returning-user split need both paths obvious.

Make entry points look like entry points (search box looks like a search box, sections like sections), and label them clearly: "Search," "Browse by Category," "Sign in," "Start here."

## Why Golden Geese make such tempting targets

Anything prominently promoted on the Home page gets a lot of extra traffic. Stakeholders see this and all want their own prominent link. It's the **tragedy of the commons** — each section gets +1 traffic from promotion, but the loss in Home-page effectiveness from clutter is shared by all.

Preserving the Home page from promotional overload requires constant vigilance, since it usually creeps in one… more… thing at a time. Educate stakeholders, and offer alternatives: cross-promote from other popular pages, take turns using prime Home-page slots.
