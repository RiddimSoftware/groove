# Chapter 1: Don't Make Me Think!

*Krug's First Law of Usability.*

## The first law

The single overriding principle: **"Don't make me think!"** When the user looks at a page, it should be self-evident. Obvious. Self-explanatory. The user should "get it" — what it is, how to use it — without having to think about it.

The bar: clear enough that your next-door neighbour, who has no interest in the site's subject and barely knows how to use the Back button, can look at the Home page and say "Oh, it's a ___."

## "Thinking" looks like question marks over the head

A self-evident page produces thought balloons like: *"OK, there's the ___. And that's a ___. And there's the thing I want."*

A page that makes you think produces: *"Hmm. Where should I start? Why did they call it that? Can I click on that? Are those two links the same thing?"*

The designer's job is to **get rid of the question marks.**

## Common sources of question marks

- **Cute, clever, marketing-induced, company-specific, or technical names.** "Jobs" beats "Employment Opportunities" beats "Job-o-Rama." Skew further toward Obvious than toward Different.
- **Links and buttons that aren't obviously clickable.** Users shouldn't spend a millisecond deciding whether something is clickable.
- **Form fields that don't give visible guidance.** Auto-suggesting "BOS — Boston Logan" as the user types beats an empty field that throws a cryptic error.

Every question mark adds cognitive load. Small individually; large in aggregate. Worse, "the people who built this didn't care enough to make it obvious" erodes trust in the site and the organisation behind it.

## Things users shouldn't have to think about

- Where am I?
- Where should I begin?
- Where did they put ___?
- What are the most important things on this page?
- Why did they call it that?
- Is that an ad or part of the site?

The point isn't to memorise this list as a checklist. It's to internalise the principle so you start noticing the question marks in *your own* daily Web use.

## Self-evident vs. self-explanatory

- **Self-evident:** the average user understands the page without any thinking.
- **Self-explanatory:** the user has to think a *little*, but the appearance, names, and small amounts of well-chosen text combine to create nearly effortless understanding.

The rule: **if you can't make it self-evident, at least make it self-explanatory.**

## Why this matters

Not because "the competition is one click away" — although that's true. The real reason is that users spend *far less* time on each page than the designer imagines. To be effective, pages have to work their magic at a glance. Self-evidence (or self-explanation) is how that happens.

Using a self-evident site feels effortless, like a store with good lighting. Using one that makes you think saps energy, enthusiasm, and time.
