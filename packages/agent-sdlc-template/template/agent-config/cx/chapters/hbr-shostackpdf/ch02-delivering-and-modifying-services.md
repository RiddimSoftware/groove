# Section 2: Delivering and Modifying Services

## Core Principles
- Services rendered by people carry a higher risk of variable quality. This must be managed by planning every encounter and instilling standards through hiring and training.
- Tangible evidence (decor, printed materials, uniforms) must be carefully orchestrated, as consumers use it to verify the service's effectiveness.
- Market research during a service's operating life is essential for measuring quality, finding new market opportunities, and modifying the blueprint.

## Enforceable Rules
- Do not leave the design of tangible evidence to chance or delegate it carelessly.
- Weigh alternate means of execution (e.g., mechanical vs. human) through a cost-benefit analysis of productivity gains against customer perceptions of quality.
- Use the blueprint to test the impact of modifications (e.g., adding a new step or product) on operations and profitability before launching them.

## Review Questions
- Does the tangible evidence (UI, branding, communications) reinforce or contradict the intended service experience?
- Have we planned the consumer-provider encounters to ensure consistent, high-quality execution?
- How will modifying this service process impact profitability, operations, and the customer's perception?

## Examples
### Violation
- An airline provides excellent transportation but neglects the appearance of its reservation desk and ticket folders, leading customers to deduce that the overall service is poor due to contradictory tangible evidence.
### Good Implementation
- A shoeshiner uses the blueprint to model adding a second coat of polish. By analyzing the blueprint, they determine this justifies a 20-cent price increase, increasing the profit margin by nearly 30% without requiring an expensive formal market test.

## Implications
### For Agents
- Agents designing user-facing applications must treat UI/UX and system messages as "tangible evidence," ensuring they consistently reflect the service's quality and style.
### For Tickets/PRs/CI
- PRs modifying a service flow must include a cost-benefit analysis of the change.
- Quality assurance must encompass not just functionality, but the consistency of the user's encounter with the system.
