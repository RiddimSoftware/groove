# Chapter 26 — Partnering with Executives

Moving to the product model often requires significant cultural change — especially when moving from top-down, command-and-control leadership styles.

> "Rather than reducing the level of interaction, in the product model, product teams need frequent, high-quality engagement."

It's not less engagement with executives. It's different engagement — engagement that **provides executives with the information they need to run the business** while **empowering product leaders and teams to do the work they're capable of**.

The chapter is organized around six dimensions of the partnership.

## Decisions

In the product model, decisions are pushed down to the product team or product leader best positioned to make them — usually those working directly with the enabling technology and the users.

But teams can only make good decisions with **strategic context**. Executives must share:
- Business strategy
- Financial parameters
- Regulatory developments
- Industry trends
- Strategic partnerships

And in return, product leaders and teams **share data and reasoning** openly so executives understand how decisions are being made.

## Outcomes

Product teams expect to be accountable for **outcomes rather than output** whenever possible.

This only works if teams are given a **problem** (not a particular solution) and are empowered to find a solution that works. Teams then promise to take responsibility for finding a solution that simultaneously serves customers, the business, and the technology.

Executives must provide **as many degrees of freedom as possible** to solve the problems.

## Disagreements

Product teams have access to enabling technology and direct interaction with customers; executives have the broadest business context. Both views are essential.

In old models, contrarian or counterintuitive ideas are not explored, and innovation rarely happens. In the product model, **counterintuitive insights often power real innovation**. Where ideas conflict, the team runs **tests** quickly and responsibly to collect evidence.

Product teams depend on executives **allowing and even encouraging** alternative approaches.

## Promises

Sometimes an executive needs to know a specific date for a specific deliverable. The teams treat these as **high-integrity commitments**.

- Product teams understand the damage done when promises are made and broken.
- **Only the team responsible for delivering** should make the promise.
- High-integrity commitments should be the **exception, not the rule** — they consume significant time and effort.
- But once made, the team pledges to take it seriously and do everything possible to deliver.

## Surprises

> "Product teams realize that surprises are sometimes unavoidable, but they also know they must try hard to minimize them."

If anything is even potentially sensitive, the PM is expected to recognize it and **discuss it with the impacted stakeholder or executive before the solution is built**.

One of the worst forms of waste is building a quality, scalable implementation only to find afterward that there's a reason it's not acceptable. Similarly, if an executive reviewed the prototype during discovery and didn't raise major issues but post-launch decides something is now a serious problem, that's also wasteful.

When something gets built but is then deemed to have a serious problem: **postmortem** to see how that type of waste can be avoided going forward.

## Trust

Empowerment depends on trust, which is earned.

- Product teams trust that executives are leading the company in a positive direction
- Executives trust that product teams are doing their best work
- Neither expects the other to be perfect
- Both commit to **best efforts to help each other succeed**

If something not viable for the business gets built or shipped, the product team promises to correct it quickly and determine how the error occurred so it isn't repeated.

PMs promise to be respectful of stakeholders' time, earn trust, be transparent. Stakeholders are **welcome and encouraged** to participate in user testing, look at prototypes, see usage data.

> "Working together, product teams and stakeholders can solve problems for your customers and your company in ways your customers could not have imagined possible."
