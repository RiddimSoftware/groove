# Chapter 15 — Product Teams

> "The most fundamental of all product concepts is the notion of an empowered, cross-functional product team. This is where effective, innovative products come from, and most of the product model is one way or another about creating and nurturing these product teams."

Four principles define product teams in the product model.

## Principle 1: Empowered with Problems to Solve

An empowered product team **exists to solve problems** in ways that customers love yet work for the business.

- The team is given **problems** (customer or company) — not features
- Their job is to find the best solution to those problems

This is the inversion of feature teams (given prioritized lists of features and projects).

A **cross-functional** product team has members covering each of the product model competencies — normally:
- A **product manager** (value + viability)
- A **product designer** (usability)
- A **tech lead** plus other engineers (feasibility, delivery)

Some refer to these three as a "product triad" or "troika." The book doesn't use those terms — sometimes coverage is by two or four people, and in the best product teams *all* engineers participate in discovery, not just the tech lead.

Additional engineers are normal: data scientists, mobile specialists, test automation / QA experts, or broadly-skilled full-stack engineers.

### Product Leaders and Empowered Teams

Empowered teams don't happen on their own.

> "In the product model, you depend on product leaders to coach the members of these product teams, and to provide them with the strategic context necessary for them to make good decisions."

Most people don't arrive at your company with the skills they'll need on an empowered product team. Even great people coming from strong product companies don't know **your** strategic context. The primary job of product leaders is to coach members to the point of competence and to remove obstacles.

## Principle 2: Outcomes over Output

> "Shipping lots of features may make you feel good, but unless that translates into real business results, you fail."

Variations: "accountable for business results," "time to money over time to market."

The principle drives different behaviors. Sometimes a team looking at data realizes the best way to improve outcomes is to **remove functionality** — common on mobile apps where screen real estate is precious and every element competes for attention. A feature-team mindset would never consider this.

## Principle 3: Sense of Ownership

For empowerment to feel real and accountability to be genuine, each team needs **a meaningful something to own**.

The team owns both:
- **Discovery** — finding the right solution
- **Delivery** — building and shipping it

Separating these into two different teams **breaks this principle** and causes serious cultural and practical problems. One of the worst transformation moves is creating a "corporate innovation lab" responsible for discovery while feature teams handle delivery — the team that discovered the solution loses its passion when it's "thrown over the wall."

Note: this doesn't mean every member spends equal time on discovery and delivery. In practice, PMs and designers spend most of their day on discovery; engineers on delivery. Ownership is shared.

Ownership scope can be a whole product or a meaningful subset — it's not unusual for a single product to have dozens or hundreds of product teams working on different aspects.

## Principle 4: Collaboration

"Collaboration" has lost its meaning through overuse. In the product model, it has a specific meaning — and is **not** how most people, especially PMs, are inclined to work.

**Collaboration is NOT:**
- **Waterfall in sprint clothing** — PM defines requirements → designer styles → engineers code
- **Consensus** — you like agreement but don't insist on it; practice *disagree and commit*
- **Democracy** — you don't vote; defer to the expert (tech lead on tech, designer on UX, PM on business constraints); when experts conflict, run a test
- **About artifacts** — PMs who write "requirements" or user stories block collaboration; once something is a "requirement," conversation ends
- **Compromise** — a mediocre UX + slow performance + dubious value is everyone losing

**Collaboration IS:** sitting around a prototype with the engineer surfacing new possibilities, designer surfacing UX trade-offs, PM weighing sales/financial/privacy implications — until you find a solution that is valuable, usable, feasible, and viable.

> "If we had to pick the one thing we love the most about the feeling of true collaboration on an empowered product team, it is the magic that happens when you have people who are motivated and skilled in their respective discipline and together they find one method that truly solves for all concerned."
