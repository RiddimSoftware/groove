# Chapter 38 — Objections from the CEO and Board

The CEO and board often have legitimate objections. How they're resolved determines whether transformation succeeds or stalls.

## "I meet with customers, investors, board members every day. How would I not be the appropriate person to decide what the product teams should deliver?" / "I am responsible for revenue, so shouldn't I define the product roadmap?"

**Response:**
- As CEO, you're indeed in a strong position. In most startups, the CEO or another cofounder is also the head of product.
- **Innovation at scale depends on empowered teams**, since customers, investors, board members, analysts, and other executives generally don't know what's just now possible.
- Some companies never transition because the CEO doesn't want to let go. As long as things go well, that may be fine — but in most cases, time forces the CEO to choose between running the product and running the company.

## "I am trying to run a business. I need to know what will be released and when."

**Response:**
- Yes — as CEO you need detailed, timely data on the progress of major product work.
- Product leaders should be able to tell you specifically **which product work is expected to contribute to which business outcomes in what time frame** — that's their product strategy.
- Cases where you need certainty: **high-integrity commitments**. But there's an opportunity cost; limit them to major work with true inherent deadlines.
- The product teams also depend on you to **share strategic context** so they can make good decisions when you're away.

## "My board wants the product roadmap."

**Response:**
- If the board doesn't understand how your product work will result in business impact, they'll often ask for a roadmap. But normally it's not the roadmap per se they care about — they want to see how the work being funded will generate the hoped-for results.
- **Product leaders connect the dots** between product work and hoped-for results.
- Pre-meet with the CEO, CFO, and CRO to ensure they all understand this chain.
- If the product organization doesn't have a track record, **trust must be earned** over time.

## "What if I have a product idea? Shouldn't I have a way to share it with the appropriate team?"

**Response:**
- Ideas from anywhere are normal and welcome in healthy organizations.
- The question is: is this a **suggestion** for one way to solve a problem, or an **edict** for what to design and build next? CEOs are often surprised to learn what they intend as suggestions are heard as edicts.
- *Be explicit.*
- Steve Jobs: "The idea is the easy part. The real work — seeing if that idea is any good, then turning that idea into a real product — is 90% of the actual work."

## "What's a reasonable timeframe to expect results? How do I know if a team is struggling vs. par for the course?"

**Response:**
- The **product strategy** spells this out, ideally in written narrative form, so all key parties understand the major work, how it plays out over the quarter and year, and how it ties to the longer-term vision.
- Assessment options: quarterly business reviews with product teams, or product leaders going deep with each team quarterly and reporting status.

## "I want our organization in alignment — product, marketing, sales, service, ops all coordinated."

**Response:**
- This is why many product-model companies use **OKRs** for annual and quarterly alignment.
- When new products release, marketing, sales, service, and ops are doing their part for success.

## "The board says my investment ratios are off — more in sales than in product."

**Response:**
- If product-market fit is established, more sales spend is correct.
- Many companies assume PMF based on employee count. Without PMF, sales spend is highly inefficient.
- Indicators: time to sell, cost to sell, conversion of trial to purchase, churn rate.
- Until PMF: focus resources on achieving it.
