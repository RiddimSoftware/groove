# Chapter 16 — Product Strategy

> "If you have an inspiring and meaningful product vision that you're all working toward achieving over the next several years, the product strategy is your path to making this product vision a reality."

> "If product teams are all about solving hard problems, then product strategy is all about how you decide which problems are most important to solve."

There are always many good opportunities and many legitimate threats. The strategy decides which ones to pursue.

## Principle 1: Focus

> "People think focus means saying yes to the thing you've got to focus on. But that's not what it means at all. It means saying no to the hundred other good ideas that there are. … Innovation is saying no to 1,000 things." — Steve Jobs

In stakeholder-driven models, focus is nearly impossible — each stakeholder has goals, and the company is trying to satisfy as many as possible.

In the product model, you look holistically at opportunities and threats; the strategy is where you **get serious about focus**.

Senior leaders must participate in focus decisions, and product leaders must be perceived as **not having a personal agenda** — hence the importance of transparency.

> "It's often less about which particular goal is declared to be the most important, and more about that a declaration has been made at all."

Most companies have several excellent goals; accomplishing even one can change the company dramatically. Chasing too many means none succeed. **Coach the CEO to select 2–3 most important goals**, then execute.

### The Power of an Inspiring Product Vision

A technique for focus: the **product vision** describes the shared future you're creating. No matter how many product teams you have, each needs to know how it contributes to the greater whole.

- Typically **3–10 years out**
- **About your customers** — how the world will be better for them
- Distinct from strategy: vision is about the customer; strategy is about the company

> "If the work or product idea in question does not take the company closer toward realizing its product vision, then you would question why this is a priority."

There may be reasons to pursue it anyway, but you'd want to be conscious that it's a detour.

## Principle 2: Powered by Insights

> "While it takes real discipline to focus, it takes real skill to identify the key insights that will power your product strategy."

Insights provide leverage points showing where to concentrate efforts. They come from:

1. **Analyzing data** — how customers use, buy, and change use of your products
2. **Talking to customers** — not asking what they want built (they don't know what's possible) but understanding their environment, what they use today, what would make them switch
3. **New enabling technologies** — what can engineers now solve that they couldn't before?
4. **The broader industry** — competitive landscape, industry trends, customer expectations changing over time

Product leaders are immersed in these insights, and the entire company is encouraged to share insights immediately. Product leaders aggregate and analyze, but insights can come from anywhere.

## Principle 3: Transparency

Strategy decisions must be visible, traceable to data, arrived at without hidden agendas. Otherwise focus discussions become political negotiations.

## Principle 4: Placing Bets

A product strategy is a set of bets — on insights, focus areas, technology timing. The discipline is making bets explicit, sized appropriately, and measured against outcomes.
