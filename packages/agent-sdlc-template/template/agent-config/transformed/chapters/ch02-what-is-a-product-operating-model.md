# Chapter 2 — What Is a Product Operating Model?

## Definition

The **product operating model** (or simply *product model*) is **not a process or a single way of working**. It is a conceptual model based on a set of first principles that strong product companies believe to be true.

> "Essentially, the product operating model is about consistently creating technology-powered solutions that your customers love, yet work for your business."

From the financial perspective: it is about getting the most out of your technology investment.

## What It Is Not

- The book did not invent the term — it observed it in use at strong product companies.
- *Product-led* and *product-centric* are common synonyms the book avoids, because they imply the product organization is taking over.
- *Customer-driven* is similarly avoided because it has been misused into meaninglessness.

## First Principles, Not Process

> "It's important to emphasize that there is no single right way to build products. There are many good ways, as there are an even larger number of bad ways."

The book authors describe themselves as **curator and evangelist** — observing what works at the best product companies, identifying common themes and first principles, and writing those down. They do not invent practices.

## The Prior Models

What you're transforming *from* is named by who is in the driver's seat:

- **IT model** — Technology serves "the business." Stakeholders define what to build.
- **Project model** — A close cousin of the IT model; CFO has outsize role because funding is project-based.
- **Feature-team model** — Stakeholders each drive a roadmap of features.
- **Sales-driven product** — Sales sets priorities.
- **Marketing-driven product** — Marketing sets priorities.

The book refers to all of these as the *prior model*, and to the destination as the *product model*.

## What Is a Product?

The book covers all types of products that fit "technology-powered products and services":

- Customer-facing apps and devices
- Internal tools used by employees
- Platform services used by other products
- Back-office data systems
- Devices (not purely software)

What's *not* a product for these purposes: third-party vendor relationships, non-technology-powered products.
