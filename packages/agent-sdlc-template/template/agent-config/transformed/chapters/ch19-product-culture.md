# Chapter 19 — Product Culture

The product culture is what makes everything else sustainable. Once built, it must be defended — there are always organizational forces pulling a company back toward command and control.

> "It takes a lot of work to establish this new product culture, but it takes very little to destroy it. You need to constantly remind yourself of the importance of the product model principles."

## Principle 1: Principles over Process

Process is what you get when you try to encode behavior in rules instead of judgment.

Strong product cultures provide context and principles, then trust people to make good decisions.

When a new process is proposed, evaluate it: does it advance the principles, or is it command-and-control with a friendly label?

## Principle 2: Trust over Control

> "Lead with context, not control." — Netflix's mantra

Empowerment requires trust:
- Leaders trust teams to find good solutions
- Teams trust leaders to provide honest strategic context
- Stakeholders trust teams to address their concerns
- Customers trust the company to keep promises

If a leader regularly disagrees with where teams land, the strategic context wasn't sufficient — that's a leadership skill to develop, not a team-empowerment problem.

## Principle 3: Innovation over Predictability

Stakeholders and CFOs want predictability. The product model produces **innovation**, which is less predictable on any single bet.

This is the deepest tension in transformation. Most failed transformations regress here: under pressure, leaders demand roadmaps and dates, which kills empowerment.

Calibration matters:
- **Release infrastructure** should be predictable (yes, we release every two weeks)
- **What a team will discover** is inherently uncertain (no, we cannot promise on Jan 1 that we'll ship X on Mar 1 producing outcome Y)

## Principle 4: Learning over Failure

Every build has two outputs: what you make and what you learn. The product model captures what you learn; the project model loses it.

A team that runs an A/B test and the new variant loses has **not failed** — they have learned something with high confidence at low cost. Treating that as a failure punishes the behavior you most need.

> "Does the company understand the necessary role of failure in innovation? Are people scared to do something that might fail? Also, does the company understand the techniques in order to fail fast and cheap so they don't fail in production?"

## Innovation Theatre

Many companies long ago lost their ability to innovate. Before deciding to transform, many tried two approaches that the book calls **innovation theatre**:

1. **Shopping for innovation via acquisitions** — most acquisitions become very expensive mistakes when factoring legacy system integration, technical debt, and unhappy customers.

2. **Corporate innovation labs** — the most common failure mode is when the lab does discovery and product teams handle delivery. This **breaks the principle that the same people who discover the solution must bring it to market**. Otherwise the passion and excitement of discovering with customers and new technology is lost when work is "thrown over the wall." Plus it creates two classes of teams: those who innovate and those who don't.
