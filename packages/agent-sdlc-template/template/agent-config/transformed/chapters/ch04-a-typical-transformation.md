# Chapter 4 — A Typical Transformation

A cautionary tale, with names removed but circumstances real.

## The Setup

A market-leading financial operations company since 2000. Strong customer focus — "delivering exactly what the customer requested" — built reputation on a long series of one-off "specials" to satisfy individual customers.

Initially successful: closing deals was the brand strength.

## The Slowdown

Despite increasing headcount and a culture of long working hours, time-to-deliver new capabilities **noticeably slowed**. New entrants gained traction quickly. Sales lagged, market share dropped.

The response: aggressive **acquisitions**. Far from igniting growth, integration proved problematic and revenue did not materialize.

## A New Start

The board replaced the CEO. A new commercially-experienced CEO arrived from management consulting with a playbook practiced across multiple sectors.

She diagnosed the issues:
- Sprawling global organization across US, India, Europe with deep cultural differences
- Acquisition-led growth had left several disconnected companies, heavy technical debt, frustrated customers

Changes: centralized org structure, **insourcing engineering**, a more focused mission, creation of a new product unit housing product managers and designers. A new **Chief Digital Officer (CDO)** was hired to lead the transformation effort.

## Friction and Pushback

- Hiring 600 engineers in a tough market: high salaries, budget exceeded, still short of the target.
- To keep costs down, **business analysts were retitled as "product managers"** instead of hiring true PMs.
- Sales — historically the sole owner of customer relationships — complained directly to the CEO.
- Incoming engineers gasped at the technical debt; each acquired business ran on its legacy stack. Leadership had little appetite for replatforming, asking engineers to "refactor where they could."

After two years: things were slower than ever. CEO decided the core issue was engineering and "needed help to deliver predictable and reliable results."

## Focus on Predictability

CIO recommended a new, **more formal, structured engineering delivery process** — longer, less frequent release cycles for predictability.

Result:
- Even fewer releases → more battleground over which BU's features got shoehorned in
- New process overhead
- A program management layer added for governance and control
- Costs continued to climb
- Competitors thrived
- Key engineers resigned, citing failure of leadership
- Customer retention degraded

## The Collapse

A small group of product and engineering leaders went directly to the CEO. Their argument: technology was preventing the company from meeting customer needs; bureaucracy was preventing forward progress.

After four years and tens of millions spent, the CEO was informed she no longer had the board's support.

> "The company's transformation had, gradually, then all at once, failed."

## Why It Failed (Implicit)

Every failure mode the rest of the book warns against:

- **Renaming roles** (business analysts → PMs) instead of building competence
- **Treating it as an IT/engineering problem** — never engaged sales, marketing, or other stakeholders
- **Predictability over innovation** — under pressure, leadership doubled down on process
- **Delegating to a CDO** who couldn't lead change across the C-suite
- **CEO not the chief evangelist** — she sponsored the work but never owned it across the company
