# Chapter 48 — Keys to Successful Transformation

The book's closing synthesis. After 47 chapters of how the product model works, what to change, what to expect, and what to watch out for — these are the common themes from successful transformations.

## 1. The Role of the CEO

> "Probably the most important lesson we have learned in 20 years of helping companies with transformations to the product model is the absolutely critical role that the CEO needs to play in order for the transformation to succeed."

- The CEO does **not** need prior product-model experience
- The CEO does **not** need to operate the transformation
- But the CEO must be the **chief evangelist** for the product model, visibly and continuously
- Delegating to a CIO, CDO, or chief transformation officer does not work because stakeholders rarely report to them

## 2. The Role of Technology

Technology must be understood as **powering the business**, not serving it.

- The IT model is a service relationship; the product model is a strategic-asset relationship
- Engineering is an innovation center, not a cost center
- Engineers must be insourced; outsourced engineers are too late and too disconnected to contribute to innovation

## 3. Strong Product Leaders

> "We use the term 'product leaders' to represent the managers of product management, product design, and engineering."

- Most companies do not have product leaders who have worked in the product model
- The book consistently observes: in every successful transformation, **the CEO took special care to ensure strong leaders of product management, product design, and engineering** — recruited if not present
- These leaders evangelize, coach, set strategic context, and remove obstacles

## 4. True Product Managers

- **Not retitled product owners or business analysts.** The PM in the product model is a different job.
- Responsible for value + viability risks and outcomes.
- Deep expertise in customers, data, business, market.
- *"Your head of product will be judged by her weakest product manager."*

## 5. Professional Product Designers

- **Not graphic designers who make things pretty at the end.**
- Skilled in interaction, service, visual, industrial design.
- First-class team members in discovery.
- Compensated at the level of PMs and engineers.

## 6. Empowered Engineers

> "Of all the principles underlying the product model, the single most important is the realization that innovation absolutely depends on empowered engineers."

- Engaged in discovery, not just delivery.
- See what is just now possible.
- Care about *what* gets built as much as *how* it gets built.

## 7. Insights-Based Product Strategy

- Not stakeholder priorities.
- Not "deliver as many features as possible."
- Focus (saying no to 1,000 things).
- Insights from data, customers, technology, industry.
- Translated to team objectives (problems to solve, outcomes to achieve).

## 8. Stakeholder Collaboration

- Not subservience to stakeholders (feature-team model).
- Not freedom from stakeholders (a common product-leader fantasy).
- **Partnership** — product teams find solutions that work for customers and business.

## 9. Continuous Evangelization of Outcomes

- Communication is constant — daily 1:1s, weekly meetings, monthly all-hands, quarterly board, ongoing customer briefings.
- The product organization tells the story of outcomes delivered, lessons learned, and the path ahead.
- Evangelism is never finished. *"There are always forces in a company that work to pull you off course."*

## 10. Corporate Courage

This is the theme that ties the rest together. The transformation is hard. There are easier paths in the short term — innovation theatre, acquisitions, predictable feature factories.

> "Successful transformation is hard to pull off, and it requires tackling tough issues where often the people involved really don't want to hear it."

Companies that succeed at transformation do so because their leaders — CEO, CFO, product leaders — have the courage to:
- Acknowledge that the current way isn't working
- Accept that change requires their own behavior to change
- Make people decisions when people aren't a fit for the new world
- Stand by the model when pressure mounts to revert
- Disrupt themselves before someone else does

## Putting It Together

Each of the transformation case studies in the book (Trainline, Datasite, Adobe) — and the innovation stories that show what becomes possible after transformation (Almosafer, Carmax, Gympass, Datasite, Kaiser Permanente, Trainline) — illustrates these ten themes.

No single one is sufficient. Combined, they are the system that strong product companies have built and that yours can build too.
