# Chapter 30 — Transformation Tactics — Competencies

Establishing the new product model competencies is usually one of the hardest parts of transformation. It is also one of the most sensitive — you are essentially asking people to learn new skills and take on additional responsibilities.

But establishing these competencies is usually **among the first things to do**: without the skills, people won't succeed with the concepts.

> "Note that many of the techniques in this chapter are described in much more detail in the book EMPOWERED."

## New Job Definitions

Many people have adopted the new titles of the product model without learning the new competencies. This problem is more severe than most companies imagine.

> "As a reminder, one of the top reasons for failed transformations is ignoring this reality and thinking you can succeed without taking the difficult step of resetting expectations of the people in the roles."

Success begins by **clearly defining the jobs that are required**. Then evaluate which of your people are capable of succeeding in those roles. With capable coaching, many can succeed — but if you don't reset job expectations, you won't see a reset in behaviors or performance.

If moving to the product model impacts job definitions outside product and engineering (e.g. project managers, business analysts), expand this work to include those roles.

## Job Reset

Occasionally, one or more competencies requires a true reset — usually when the company has been using the same title (e.g. "product manager") for what was really a different job.

The recommended technique: **temporarily rename all your current product managers** to "product analysts" or "product specialists." Then as people interview for or get trained in the newly defined role, use only the new "product manager" title once the person has demonstrated capability in the new role.

This signals the rest of the company that the role has changed **very intentionally**.

## Role Balancing

When moving to the product model, companies typically end up needing:
- **Fewer but stronger** product managers
- **A larger number of more broadly skilled** product designers
- **A strong set** of engineering tech leads

Product leaders often have a steep learning curve because their role is much different in the product model.

Typical empowered product team: 1 PM, 1 designer, 2–10 engineers. Platform team: 1 (very technical) PM, 4–20 engineers. **Prefer fewer, larger teams over many small ones.**

## Dealing with Too Few Designers

Common gap. Not sustainable, but three bridges:
1. **Triage** — assign designers only to teams most in need.
2. **Contract designers** — month-long freelancers, not project-tied.
3. **One designer across multiple teams** — drops off dramatically beyond two teams.

## Dealing with Outsourced Engineers

Moving to the product model **absolutely requires insourcing engineers**.

> "Just as you wouldn't outsource your CEO, you wouldn't outsource your key engineers."

- Insourcing takes real time. **The tech lead must be insourced first and immediately**. *"You don't have a product team unless you have a tech lead."*
- That tech lead can then coordinate with other engineers (some still outsourced) until they too are insourced.
- A smaller insourced team consistently outperforms a much larger outsourced team. Insourcing almost always results in **cost savings** *plus* dramatically improved innovation.
- If your organization isn't serious about insourcing engineering, it isn't serious about transformation.

## Raising Engagement Level of Engineers

Sometimes engineers have been treated as mercenaries so long they say they have no desire to participate in product discovery.

- Put discovery responsibility **explicitly in the tech lead's job description**.
- Bring engineers along on **customer visits** — *"it is remarkable the impact that can have."*

## Product Managers and Line-of-Business Managers

In domains where a PM partners with a category manager / editor / general manager of a business unit:
- The PM owns the holistic digital/omnichannel experience.
- The line-of-business counterpart owns the underlying content or service.
- **Watch out**: business counterparts often want PMs reduced to order-takers, returning to stakeholder-driven roadmaps.

If the issue is that the PM doesn't actually have the skills, sometimes the right answer is to **let the business counterpart take on the PM role**, assuming they can be coached into the new competency.

## New Recruiting Practices

Moving to the product model makes your company **more attractive** to the candidates you need to hire.

- Hiring managers must own recruiting; HR supports.
- Interview teams must know what to look for and what the candidate is assessing.

## Assessments and Coaching Plans

Once people are in the right spots, assess each against the skills required to succeed, identify gaps, and build a coaching plan.

## Onboarding Programs

Reading a book or attending a workshop is one thing; learning hands-on is another. Create a **product model onboarding program** — ideally attended by product teams together, with key stakeholders dropping in at relevant moments.
