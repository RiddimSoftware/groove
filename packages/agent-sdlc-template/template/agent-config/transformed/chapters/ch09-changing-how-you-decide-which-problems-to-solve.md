# Chapter 9 — Changing How You Decide Which Problems to Solve

The third — and typically most profound — transformation dimension.

## The Question Behind the Question

Getting skilled at discovery is a leap. But it doesn't answer:

> "How did you decide this was the most important problem to be solved?"

In prior models, stakeholders prioritize problems as part of an annual product planning process. In a company that has moved to the product model, problems come from an **insight-driven product strategy**.

## Disrupting Product Planning

In stakeholder-driven feature-team companies, there's typically an annual product-planning process. Stakeholders make their cases. In some companies finance arbitrates business cases; in others, senior leadership decides.

Companies that genuinely care about accountability track delivered results versus promises made. Neither of these approaches consistently produces good decisions because **they don't have good data going in**.

> "Product planning is about deciding which problems you need to solve. Changing how you decide which problems to solve is the third dimension of moving to the product model."

## Disrupting Yourselves Before You're Disrupted

It's easy to overlook how fundamental this change is.

Suppose you know that due to competitive pressures and changes in customer needs, you need a substantially new generation of your core product. The changes will run deep — impacting how products are created, marketed, sold, delivered, and serviced.

No matter how many offsites you hold or consultants you bring in, do you really believe each stakeholder will do what's necessary to disrupt themselves?

And even if they wanted to, the answer rarely sits in the conference room. **It is very challenging to innovate meaningfully that far away from the technology and the customer.**

The product model understands this. With CEO support, product leaders and product teams work with stakeholders to drive necessary changes across the organization.

This dynamic plays out vividly in each of the three transformation stories — Trainline, Datasite, Adobe.

## Customer-Centric Product Vision

The product vision describes the future you're trying to create and is the foundation for the strategy.

- **About the customer** — how it will make users' and customers' lives better
- **Not about you** — not how you'll make more money, not your quarterly priorities, not the team structure
- **3–10 years out**
- **Unites teams** with a shared goal of making the vision a reality
- **Best recruiting tool** for strong product people

## Insight-Driven Product Strategy

While the vision describes the future, the strategy is how you identify the most important problems to solve **now**.

The strategy begins with **focus** — *"the main thing is to keep the main thing the main thing"* (Jim Barksdale). Most companies dilute their efforts across too many goals and make too little progress on each.

Once focus areas are identified, **insights** drive the strategy:
- Quantitative insights from data
- Qualitative insights from customer conversations
- Continuous evaluation of new enabling technologies
- Industry and competitive trends

> "Good product strategy is a force multiplier."

Product strategy is distinct from business strategy and go-to-market strategy. Most companies have those; **product strategy is often missing completely** — replaced with "deliver as many features as possible," which is not a strategy.

## The Role of Product Leadership

The third dimension is the one where strong **product leaders distinguish themselves**. The other dimensions can be coached. Strategy can't be outsourced.

- They live and breathe the data and insights
- Constantly seek leverage points
- A strong product strategy lets a small organization outperform much larger competitors
- The output: business or customer problems to solve (team objectives) assigned to specific teams
