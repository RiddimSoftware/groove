# Chapter 46 — Objections from Inside Product

The hardest objections often come from inside the product organization itself.

## "Stakeholders just need to back off and give us space to do our job."

**Response:**
- No. Empowered teams require *more* engagement with stakeholders, not less. See [Chapter 26 — Partnering with Executives](ch26-partnering-with-executives.md).
- The asks of stakeholders are different: bring problems not solutions, share strategic context, disagree and commit.
- *"Lead with context, not control"* applies to product leaders too — and they must hear it.

## "Engineers don't want to engage in discovery."

**Response:**
- It's a **job-description issue**. Put discovery responsibility explicitly in the tech lead's responsibilities.
- Bring engineers to **customer visits** — the impact is remarkable.
- Sometimes the issue is engineers being treated as mercenaries for so long they've internalized the role. Coach them out of it.

## "My CEO won't stop demanding roadmaps."

**Response:**
- Some of that is the CEO's job to change.
- Some is the **product leader's job to change their CEO's mind** — see "Tough Love for Product Leaders" in [Chapter 6](ch06-a-guide-to-transformed.md).
- Tactical paths: outcome-based roadmaps that translate the existing roadmap items into problems and outcomes, then a quarterly review that compares predicted outcomes to actual results. This builds CEO trust over time.

## "Designers are stretched too thin."

**Response:**
- Triage — assign designers to teams most in need.
- Contract designers for months-long bridges.
- Never split a designer across more than two teams (contribution drops dramatically).
- Hire more — they're available if you compete on compensation and project mission.

## "We don't have time for discovery — we have a backlog."

**Response:**
- Discovery isn't extra work; it's **the work**.
- Without it, the backlog is being built blind, and 70–90% of what's built doesn't generate ROI (per industry data).
- Discovery is what causes the **ratio of tested-to-built ideas to be more than 2:1** — more discarded ideas than built ones.

## "We tried this and it didn't work."

**Response:**
- "Tried" often means "implemented half of it" — usually the easy half (titles, ceremonies).
- The product model is a **whole system**. Adopting empowered teams without changing release cadence, or changing release cadence without changing how problems are decided, predictably underperforms.
- An honest assessment (Chapter 29) shows what was actually adopted vs. what was skipped.

## "We need more PMs."

**Response:**
- Usually you need **fewer but stronger PMs**, not more. The product model uses competent PMs efficiently across larger team scopes.
- If you don't have strong PMs, hire from outside or coach from inside. Don't dilute by adding more weak ones.

## "Our product owners are doing fine."

**Response:**
- This is the most dangerous objection because it justifies inaction.
- Symptoms of product owners (vs. PMs): all decisions escalate to manager or stakeholder meeting; PM is in meetings all day with no customer contact; product team can't be held accountable for outcomes.
- If you see these symptoms, your product owners are not "doing fine" — they're a transformation failure waiting to be acknowledged.
