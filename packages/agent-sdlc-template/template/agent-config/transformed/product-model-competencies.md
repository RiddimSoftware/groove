# Product Model Competencies

A distillation of Part III of *TRANSFORMED* — the new skills and capabilities the company must build in its people before the product operating model is achievable.

## Why Competencies Come Before Concepts

> "Unless you are willing to establish these new competencies, your transformation hopes will likely end here."

Most companies that transform underestimate the gap between the titles they already have ("product manager," "designer," "engineering lead") and the **competencies** the product model requires. Cosmetic relabeling — retitling business analysts to product managers — guarantees failure.

The competencies are the foundation. Strong products are created by strong product teams, and strong product teams are staffed with these four roles done well.

## The Four Risks the Product Team Must Address

In the product model, an empowered product team is given a problem to solve and must produce a solution that customers love yet works for the business. The team must address four risks:

| Risk | Question | Primary owner |
|---|---|---|
| **Value risk** | Will the customer buy our solution or choose to use it? | Product manager |
| **Viability risk** | Will this solution work for our business? (marketable, sellable, serviceable, fundable, monetizable, legal) | Product manager |
| **Usability risk** | Can users easily learn, use, and perceive the value? | Product designer |
| **Feasibility risk** | Do we know how to build and scale this with the staff, time, technology, and data we have? | Tech lead |

All three competencies can and do contribute to all aspects of the solution. The risk taxonomy clarifies **who is held accountable** for what.

(IDEO uses an alternate taxonomy — desirability, feasibility, viability — that collapses value and usability into "desirability." TRANSFORMED finds this works for consumer products but fails for B2B, where "can a user use this" and "will a company buy this" are very different questions.)

## Competency 1: Product Manager

> "We will warn you now that this is consistently the most difficult new competency to establish."

This is the role most commonly faked. Your company likely already has people titled "product manager" — but in the prior model, that title often meant "product owner" (an Agile ceremony role), "business analyst" (a requirements writer), or "program manager" (a scheduler). Those are completely different jobs.

### What a product manager does

The product manager is **explicitly responsible for the value and viability** of the solutions built, and is **overall accountable for achieving the product's outcomes**. This is a different mandate from feature delivery.

To do this, a PM must develop expertise in:

- **Users and customers** — qualitatively and quantitatively. How do they choose your product? How do they use it? What do they want that they cannot articulate?
- **Data** — direct, unencumbered access to product analytics, behavioral data, and business data.
- **The business** — funding, monetization, manufacturing, marketing, sales, delivery, service, plus legal/contractual/compliance constraints.
- **The market** — competitive landscape, technology trends, industry dynamics.

A new PM typically needs **2–3 months** with active coaching to come up to speed. Without coaching, they may sit in the role for years and never reach competence.

### The bar for hiring/promoting

- *"Your head of product will be judged by her weakest product manager."*
- *"Your CEO should believe that each of your product managers has the potential to be a future leader of your company in the next five or so years."*

Most legacy business analysts and Agile-focused product owners do not reach this bar. Their roles are narrowly defined; the PM role is broad and deep.

### Symptoms of an incompetent PM

The clearest sign: every decision requires either escalation to a manager or a stakeholder meeting where stakeholders are asked to decide. A competent PM makes decisions — informed by data, customer empathy, business understanding, and discussion with engineers and designers.

### Where to look for talent inside the company

Look for:
- Broad understanding of the business, proven ability to learn other parts
- Comfortable immersing in data
- Wants to talk to real users
- Willing to whiteboard with designers and engineers on solutions that satisfy many constraints
- Loves to learn fast and collaborate

These people exist scattered across your organization. Identify them, get them into the role, and provide intensive coaching.

### Domain expertise: nuance

Domain expertise is conspicuously **not** listed as a primary attribute. Companies often hire PMs from their customer base on the theory that "they know our domain." This usually backfires:

- *Too little* domain knowledge → struggles with the job.
- *Too much* domain knowledge → **domain dogma**. Shreyas Doshi's framing: "true domain expertise is domain knowledge minus domain dogma." Dogma is "all the reasons it won't work."

New PMs are expected to become expert in the domain in their first months. For domains requiring deep expertise (surgical instruments, tax preparation, regulatory reporting), hire a dedicated domain expert shared across teams.

### Direct access (non-negotiable)

The PM must have direct, unencumbered access to:

1. **Users and customers** — *no one* should be allowed to gate this. Not sales, not customer success, not user researchers, not vendor managers. Train teams on appropriate interactions and let them go.
2. **Product data** — the PM is flying blind without it. Privacy/governance can be engineered around without an intermediary.
3. **Business stakeholders** — marketing, sales, services, finance, legal, compliance, manufacturing, SMEs. The PM must establish trust with each, learn their constraints, and ensure they are addressed before the team builds anything in the gray viability area.

Engineers should also have direct user access. "The magic happens" when an engineer watches a user struggle with the product.

## Competency 2: Product Designer

The role is **not** "make things pretty after the PM and engineers decide what to build." That is the prior model.

A product designer in the product model:

- **Owns the holistic customer experience** — every interaction the user has with the product, end to end, across channels.
- Is responsible for **usability risk** and accountable for the **product's experience**.
- Designs **changes** to the experience intentionally — so users barely notice updates, or if they do, the experience guides them through.
- Is skilled in **interaction design** (how users and technology communicate), plus service, visual, and (for devices) industrial design.

### Designer as discovery partner

The single most important shift: the designer is the **key partner for the PM and tech lead in daily product discovery work**.

- The designer takes the lead in expressing product ideas as **prototypes** — often a dozen or more per week.
- Most prototypes are thrown away. Each one converges the team on an effective solution.

### Compensation reality

Strong product designers are compensated at the level of a PM and an engineer. The market demand for this caliber of designer continues to grow as more companies move to the product model. Each user-facing product team normally has a dedicated product designer.

## Competency 3: Tech Lead

Note: "Tech lead" is the book's term for the **most senior individual contributor engineer** on the team. The title used varies (staff engineer, principal engineer, lead engineer); the title is not important — the mandate is.

A tech lead is normally an IC, not an engineering manager. The team typically has multiple engineers of mixed experience; the tech lead is the senior one.

### Two dimensions distinguish product-model engineers from IT-model engineers

1. **Complexity of the work.** Product companies routinely face scale, performance, fault tolerance, reliability, internationalization, test automation, deployment infrastructure, emerging technologies, and managing technical debt. Mistakes in these areas are very expensive. Experience matters.

2. **Engagement in problem-solving.** This is the more important dimension and the source of real innovation.

### The single most important principle in the product model

> "Of all the principles underlying the product model, the single most important is the realization that innovation absolutely depends on empowered engineers."

Engineers work with the enabling technology every day. They are uniquely positioned to see **what is just now possible**. But this only translates to innovation if engineers care just as much about *what* they build as *how* they build it.

The old adage — *"PM owns the what and the why; engineers own the how"* — completely misses the point of empowered product teams.

### The tech lead's discovery mandate

If the tech lead is unable or unwilling to engage in product discovery, the eventual product will not achieve your goals. Period.

This must be a formal part of the tech lead's job description. In practice it does not require much time — typically less than an hour a day. But that hour, every day, is what separates the product model from the project model.

> "A few minutes weighing in on a product idea very early can save weeks or even months of damage control later."

### Why outsourced engineering is incompatible with the product model

If your engineers are external — they are quite literally mercenaries.

- Outsourced engineers are brought in too late, with too little context, to participate in discovery.
- The product team loses the "what is just now possible" channel entirely.
- The team is reduced to a feature team that runs slower and produces less, and you would be better off hiring Accenture, who is better at that than you are likely to be.

The book is direct: if your prior model relied heavily on outsourcing, **insourcing engineers** is one of the highest-leverage transformation moves. Almosafer (Chapter 14) is the case study for this.

## Competency 4: Product Leaders

Product leaders are the **managers of product management, product design, and engineering** — the people who recruit, onboard, coach, and develop the PMs, designers, and engineers.

> "Empowered product teams depend on better leadership, not less leadership."

A common misconception is that empowerment means leaders back off. The opposite is true — empowerment requires *more* skilled leadership.

### Andy Grove's framing

> "What gets in the way of good work? There are only two possibilities. The first is that people don't know how to do good work. The second is that they know how, but they aren't motivated."

Product leaders address both: management (skill) and leadership (motivation and direction).

### Management responsibility 1: Coaching

> "It is the single most important responsibility of every people manager."

Coaching is the single most overlooked element of strong management. It does NOT mean micromanaging. It means:
- Assessing strengths and weaknesses
- Building a coaching plan
- Spending the quality time necessary to help each person improve

In strong product orgs, engineers report to experienced engineering managers, designers to design managers, PMs to proven product management leaders. First-level managers spend up to **80% of their workweek** on staffing and coaching.

### Management responsibility 2: Staffing

Managers — not HR — are accountable for sourcing, recruiting, interviewing, onboarding, evaluating, promoting, and replacing team members.

- HR supports; managers own.
- *"Setting the bar high in our approach to hiring has been, and will be, the single most important element of Amazon's success."* — Jeff Bezos

### Leadership: two ways to lead

1. **Command and control** — telling people what to do via roadmaps of features and projects. Leaders and stakeholders make decisions; teams execute. *Easier to do.*
2. **Empowering the teams** — assigning business or customer problems and letting teams find the best way to solve them.

Empowerment requires providing **strategic context**: product vision and product strategy. *"Lead with context, not control."* — Netflix's mantra.

If a leader finds herself disagreeing with where the empowered teams land, or swooping in late to change direction, that is a signal that the up-front context was insufficient. This is a skill that requires reflection and improvement.

### What product leaders own that teams do not

- **Product vision** — the 3–10 year future you are creating; the most powerful recruiting tool.
- **Team topology** — how teams are structured, what they own, how they relate. Maximizes empowerment by being loosely coupled but highly aligned. Avoid Conway's-Law accidents.
- **Product strategy** — the focus areas, the insights, the bets.
- **Team objectives** — typically quarterly. The litmus test for empowerment: the team can decide the best way to solve the problems they were given (their objectives), and proposes key results back to leaders.

### Ongoing evangelism

The final critical role of product leaders is **constant communication** of the product vision, team topology, and product strategy — to the product organization and across the company.

> "The larger the organization, the more essential it is to be relentless at evangelism, and it's important for the leaders to understand that evangelism is something that never finished. It needs to be constant."

## Product Ops: The Supporting Function

In some companies, user researchers and data analysts are organized into a small **product ops** group that **coaches and supports** product teams.

Done well, product ops:
- Brings expert testing techniques to teams that lack them
- Ensures insights flow back to product leaders for future strategy
- Lets the product teams retain direct customer access while accelerating their decisions

### Anti-patterns to watch for

- Product ops as a *gatekeeper* between teams and users/data/stakeholders. *Be careful that's not the case for you.*
- Product ops as a rebranded PMO (program management office) — old command-and-control culture in new clothes.

The prior model does not usually go away without a fight, and PMO-style "product ops" is one of its favorite disguises.

## AI's Impact on These Competencies

AI tooling has improved productivity for PMs, designers, and engineers — especially engineers — by automating tedious tasks and accelerating idea evaluation. The book expects this to continue.

But AI raises the stakes on **ethical and business-viability risks**. Product teams can now do more than ever, but more carefully. Strong product teams continuously evaluate how AI helps build products, just as they evaluate how AI helps customers.

## Other Impacted Roles

Two other functions are significantly impacted by the transition:

- **Product marketing** — discussed in [Chapter 23](chapters/ch23-partnering-with-product-marketing.md).
- **Project management / delivery management** — its scope shrinks dramatically once teams own their delivery.

## Summary: What "Title" Is Not Enough

> "Many people have adopted the new titles of the product model without actually learning the new competencies. This problem is much more severe than you might imagine."

The implication for transformation:

1. Audit your existing PMs, designers, engineers, and product leaders against the bar in this document. Be honest. Expect to find that **most do not yet have the necessary competence**.
2. Decide who can be coached up and who cannot. Some will need other roles where they will be more successful.
3. Hire from outside where needed — especially for product leadership, which sets the bar for everyone else.
4. Build coaching capacity. Without coaches, none of this scales.

Without this honest audit and the willingness to act on it, you have a transformation in name only.
